What this is — and what it is NOT

Read it: Start here. The console is explicitly NOT predictive. It is a stress thermometer, not a crash detector.
Does NOT mean: Do NOT use this to time trades or forecast crashes. Do NOT assume high readings mean a collapse is imminent. Do NOT treat lead-lag associations as causal relationships.

The Honesty Contract. This console describes financial stress in real time. It is not a crash predictor or a timing tool, and it makes no forecast of crashes or of price direction. The separate Predictive tab shows only the small set of things that are genuinely forecastable — short-horizon volatility, months-horizon recession and credit context from leading indicators such as the yield curve and credit spreads, and decade-scale valuation — each shown with its limits, and none of them is a trading signal. The framing borrows from physics (critical slowing down, phase transitions) as an analogy only — these signals are mostly absent before historical meltdowns.

Why Markets Aren't Like the Water System. The console's core panels — spectral radius, absorption ratio, turbulence, Hawkes clustering, lead-lag — were built as a thermometer of stress, not a thermometer of future crisis. By their own authors' admission, they rise during 2008/2020/2022, not before. Markets invert every advantage the water system has:

  • Topology is estimated and rewires in a crisis. The cross-asset network here is inferred from noisy returns; the instant volatility spikes, correlations collapse to one mode (everything moves together), and the graph rewires unpredictably.
  • Nothing is conserved. A crowded position unwinds in milliseconds through price. There is no quantity to integrate forward — no water level rising to a calculable overflow.
  • Drivers are endogenous and already priced. The would-be exogenous shocks (Fed policy, CPI, flows) are already embedded in price expectations. Only the unforecastable surprise remains.
  • Information has ~zero transport delay. Any stable upstream-leads-downstream pattern gets arbitraged away immediately. Lead-lag is an association observed in the data, not a causal edge you can exploit.
  • Price is the expectation itself. There is no hidden state. Watching the gauge perturbs it (reflexivity). The market is not a system with secrets to uncover.

What the Panels Actually Tell You. When the Fragility Thermometer rises (60–100), you are seeing that an asset's stress indicators have been rising unusually fast for it over the last 60 days; when spectral radius climbs or Mahalanobis turbulence spikes, you are seeing that the market is currently moving in ways that are statistically large or tightly coupled — today. A high reading can persist for months or years with nothing catastrophic happening. It can also drop from low levels with no warning. This is coincident, not leading.

The Only Weakly-Honest Leading Signal. The standardized absorption-ratio shift has shown the barest hint of lead in some sample windows — but it is sample-dependent and has not held out-of-sample. Do not trade on it.

Header: Threat Tier Pill

The coloured pill at the top right names the tier of the single highest Fragility-thermometer score across all 51 markets (the score itself, and which market it is, are in the banner below the header):

  • Green, "Resilient" (0–59): the asset's fragility indicators are not rising unusually fast for it. This says nothing about their level: an already-volatile asset that is no longer getting more so can sit here.
  • Amber, "Reduced resilience — watch" (60–84): its fragility indicators have been rising faster than is usual for that asset over the last 60 days, but not extremely so.

The pill refreshes whenever the console recomputes — on load, after a live refresh that changed prices, and whenever you move the Backtest cursor or run a drill. It is a coincident indicator — it moves when stress is happening now, not before it arrives.

Live | Backtest Toggle

The two-button toggle in the header switches between two modes:

  • Live monitor (default): starts from the embedded snapshot and tries to refresh every series once at load and then every 10 minutes, asking each feed for data from that series' snapshot end onward (crypto from Binance.US, FX from Frankfurter, VIX and all 43 US-listed stocks and ETFs from Yahoo via a proxy). A live tail is never joined across a hole of more than 7 calendar days: that feed is reported as a gap and its series stay on the snapshot (see Where the data comes from). If a refresh changes no prices, nothing is recomputed; if it does, most — not all — panels recompute (see Live vs Backtest mode).
  • Backtest / research: adds the Backtest replay & base-rate honesty card with a date cursor. The walk-forward panels then use only data up to the cursor date; a few panels do not follow the cursor, and the manifold has look-ahead (see Live vs Backtest mode). A separate "base-rate honesty" table does not follow the cursor: over the selected asset's whole history, it compares how often a ≥10% drawdown followed within 60 trading days on high-fragility (≥70) days versus on any day. Because crashes are rare (handful per decade), any apparent "skill" is mostly luck.

In plain English — what the latest data says (start here)

Read it: The briefing card (top of the right-hand column on desktop, further down on phones; on every tab) auto-writes 6–8 plain sentences describing what the panels are saying — for a non-specialist. The header also shows a one-line regime summary.
Does NOT mean: "Elevated" or "Stressed" is a description of current conditions, never a prediction or a buy/sell signal.

If you read nothing else, read this panel. It translates the whole console into plain language and updates with the data (and with the date cursor in Backtest). Its heading says which date it describes: "Right now" when the data is fresh; "As of 2026-10-20 (embedded snapshot · N days old)" when the data is more than 4 days old and no live feed updated anything; "As of YYYY-MM-DD (oldest series end · N days old)" when live feeds updated some series but the oldest series end is still more than 4 days old; or the cursor date in Backtest. The bullets use time words that match the heading: "Today's" and "right now" only when the data is fresh and no cursor is set; otherwise "The 2026-10-20 snapshot's" and "in the latest snapshot data" (or "The latest shared day's (YYYY-MM-DD)" and "in the latest data" once live feeds have updated some series), and "That day's" and "as of that date" in Backtest. When the data is stale, the per-market readings (the breadth behind the regime word and the most stretched name) are taken on the date the heading prints; if the most stretched market has a newer bar, the first sentence says so and notes that the banner shows each market's newest bar.

The header regime line

Under the title you'll see, e.g., Regime: Slightly elevated · coupling moderate · turbulence calm · data as of 2026-10-20 (in Backtest the last part reads cursor YYYY-MM-DD). That's the one-glance summary: the overall regime word, whether assets are moving together (coupling — high at or above the 65th percentile of its own history, moderate from the 40th, otherwise low), whether today's combined move is unusual (turbulence — high at ≥85, elevated at ≥60, otherwise calm), and the date the cross-asset data runs to.

The regime word is systemic, not one stock

The headline regime — Calm / Slightly elevated / Elevated — watch / Stressed — is driven by the system: how tightly assets are coupled, how large the combined move is, and how many assets are individually elevated. Stressed means turbulence ≥85, coupling at or above its 85th percentile, or at least 45% of markets with fragility ≥60; Elevated — watch uses 60 / 65th / 25%; Slightly elevated uses 45 / 45th / 12%; otherwise Calm. A single very-stretched name does not turn the whole headline red (that name is still called out in the first sentence). The colored pill in the top-right corner, by contrast, flags the single least-resilient asset.

What each sentence covers

  • Overall + most stretched name — the regime, and which single market's fragility indicators have been rising fastest relative to its own history (a 60-day trend, not a level: an already-volatile market that is no longer getting more so is not "stretched").
  • Coupling — are markets moving independently or together, and the plain consequence: when they move together, diversification protects you less.
  • Absorption-ratio shift (only when it is at least 1σ above its baseline) — the way markets share risk is reorganising; the one construct with a weak tendency to lead, sample-dependent and coincident in practice.
  • Turbulence — is today's combined move ordinary or unusually large.
  • Leaders — only when more directed pairs pass the network's significance gate than chance alone would give (the same test as the network card's footer: more than 1.2 × the ~5% of pairs that pass by chance): which market's moves have tended to come first lately, and the gate-passing market it leads most strongly. It flags when that market is an international ETF, whose US-hours "lead" is partly mechanical, and adds the reminder that this is a pattern, not a cause, and it can flip. Otherwise it says the network finds no lead-lag structure beyond chance, with the count of passing pairs next to what chance gives (64 of 1,275 against ~64 on the embedded snapshot), or, if the top net transmitter has no gate-passing link, that no single market is clearly leading. If the network has too little shared history at the date (Backtest before about 2023-10-09; crypto history starts 2023-09-21), it says there is not enough shared history at that date to tell whether any market leads, instead of reading the empty network as simultaneity.
  • Closest historical analog — which past date today's market "shape" most resembles (a resemblance, not a prediction).
  • Shock clustering — whether big moves are isolated or clustering.
  • Anticipation — which market the full-history spillover decomposition ranks as the top net exporter of stress (the Diebold–Yılmaz transmitter), whether its out-of-sample lead beats the basket's own stress persistence (on the embedded snapshot it does not, so the sentence calls it a place to look, not a proven leader), and whether that ranking is stable — association, not cause, and not a trade signal.

Every readout ends with the same bottom line: it describes conditions on the date in the heading ("today" only when the data is fresh; when the data is stale it adds "not today" and says either that live feeds did not update the snapshot or that they have not brought every series up to date), it does not forecast a crash, and it is not advice.

Directed spillover network (LCCA) — who leads whom

Read it: Diagnostic tab, first card. Left = net transmitters (red), right = net receivers (blue). Only the 12 strongest asymmetries are drawn; among them, solid arrows passed the permutation gate (the small label on each is the lag, in days, of its peak) and dashed ones were drawn but fell below significance. The footer counts how many of the 12 drawn links pass, and how many of all 1,275 directed pairs pass overall — about 5% would pass by chance alone, because each pair is tested separately with no multiple-testing correction, so that count is not by itself evidence of structure. Most surviving leads peak at 1 day — effectively contemporaneous. Lead-lag here is association, not causation.
Does NOT mean: Not causal graphs. Not a predictor of spillover events. Not a ranking of systemic importance—transmitters may be volatility conduits, not fundamental drivers. The network rewires in crises; historical in-sample links often don't hold out-of-sample. Lead-lag is dominated by common risk factors and information lags, not structural causality.

What Is LCCA?

Lagged Cross-Correlation Asymmetry: measures whether one asset's past returns line up with another's future returns more than the reverse. An arrow i→j means i's lagged returns correlate more strongly with j's forward returns than the reverse, over the last 252 trading days that all 51 markets share.

Calculation

For each pair i, j and lags k = 1 to L (L = 4 days):

  • Compute Fisher z-transformed cross-correlation: xcorr(i, j, k) at lag k.
  • Average absolute z-values: D(i→j) = Σ|fz(xcorr(i, j, k))| / L.
  • Asymmetry: A(i→j) = D(i→j) − D(j→i). Red i→j means i leads j; blue j leads i; near-white = pure co-movement.
  • Net transmitter/receiver: NET(i) = Σⱼ A(i→j). Positive = transmitter (red), negative = receiver (blue).

Permutation Gate

Each of 160 null draws circularly shifts every series by its own random offset — destroying the lead-lag timing while keeping each series' own behaviour — and recomputes the asymmetries. A link passes only if its asymmetry exceeds the 95th percentile of that pair's 160 null values. The draws use a fixed seed, so the same data always gives the same gate. The diagram draws only the 12 strongest positive asymmetries: solid = passed the gate; faint dashed = drawn but below significance. Many pairs that pass are not drawn. The footer therefore gives two counts — how many of the 12 drawn links pass the gate (solid), and how many of all directed pairs pass overall — next to the reminder that about 5% would pass by chance: each pair is tested on its own at the 5% level with no multiple-testing correction, so across 1,275 pairs some 60-odd passes are expected from noise alone.

Directedness Ratio

Σ|A| / ΣD—how much of the total cross-correlation is directional (asymmetric) vs co-movement. Higher = clearer leader-follower structure; lower = assets moving as one, simultaneously. The console computes it only for the current window (no history of it is plotted), so this guide makes no claim about how it behaves through crises; the common-shock drill in the Scenario sandbox shows that adding simultaneous moves with no leader lowers it or leaves it about where it was (it plants no leader), while the inject-lead drill raises it. In Backtest, until the 51-market network has at least 12 shared days (crypto history starts 2023-09-21, so before about 2023-10-09), the ratio reads n/a and the footer says there is too little shared history at that date.

Critical Honesty Note

Lead-lag is association, not causation. Most surviving leads peak at 1 day (≈contemporaneous), reflecting information diffusion and microstructure, not causal transmission. With the Scenario sandbox you can inject a synthetic lead to watch the network respond (in Live, or in Backtest from about 2023-10-09 on; before that the 51-market network has fewer than the 12 shared days it needs (crypto history starts 2023-09-21), so its panels say "not enough shared history at this date"; the drills still change coupling, absorption, turbulence and Hawkes, which use the 48 non-crypto markets, and the DRILL — SYNTHETIC markers stay on)—but real lead-lag tendencies are fleeting and don't generalize out-of-sample. The network is a snapshot of dependence structure, not a transmission mechanism.

Asymmetry matrix & net transmitter / receiver

Read it: The N×N heatmap (rows = source, columns = target) shows which asset leads which. The bar chart to the right ranks all assets by net directedness: red extends right (transmits), blue extends left (receives).
Does NOT mean: The values are correlation-based associations, not causal drivers. A red SPY→LQD link does not mean stock strength causes bond weakness; it means their lagged returns moved together in that direction during the window — and only links drawn solid on the network diagram passed the circular-shift gate; matrix cells are not gated.

The left panel shows the directed asymmetry matrix A(i→j) = D(i→j) − D(j→i), where D(i→j) is the average absolute (Fisher-z) lagged cross-correlation of i leading j over lags 1–4.

Matrix interpretation:

  • Red cells (positive A) indicate i→j: asset i leads asset j.
  • Blue cells (negative A) indicate j→i: asset j leads asset i.
  • Near-white cells indicate pure co-movement with no directional winner (both lead/lag symmetrically).

The matrix is anti-symmetric: if A(SPY→LQD) is red, then A(LQD→SPY) is equally blue. Row and column labels use the ticker (FX pairs by their quote currency, e.g. JPY).

Right panel — net directional spillover: A table ranks assets by their NET score (sum of all outgoing leads minus all incoming lags), with a centred bar:

  • Right/red bars: Pure sources and hubs that lead more than they lag.
  • Left/blue bars: Pure sinks and relays that lag more than they lead.
  • TO[i] = total outgoing lead-lag contribution (i leads others).
  • FROM[i] = total incoming lead-lag contribution (others lead i).

Which markets transmit and which receive changes with the window, so there is no stable "typical" pattern to memorise. On the embedded snapshot (markets as of 2026-10-20), NVDA, international corporate bonds (IBND) and SOL are the largest net transmitters, and the strongest receivers are the FX pairs (USD vs AUD, EUR and CHF).

Cascade simulation — shock propagation

Read it: Pick a market to shock and a minimum link strength; the panel propagates the shock downstream along the directed spillover network and reports how many markets it reaches, in how many waves, and which fall in each wave. It also reports the percolation threshold — the strongest link-strength cut at which a shock from the chosen market still reaches at least half of all markets.
Does NOT mean: NOT a forecast and NOT a claim that shocking X will hit Y. The network is estimated from noisy returns, it rewires in a crisis, and lead-lag is association arbitraged toward lag 0. This is a descriptive what-if drill on the current inferred structure.

This is your St. Pete water‑cascade mechanic — directed downstream propagation — brought to the market network as a directed‑reachability (percolation) cascade, the same family of model used for epidemics, power‑grid blackouts, and bank runs. A shock at the source flows to any market reachable via directed lead‑lag links of at least the chosen strength; each wave is one hop further out. Raising the trigger keeps only the strongest links (the cascade localises); lowering it lets weaker links carry the shock system‑wide. The behaviour is genuinely source‑dependent and moves with the window: on the embedded snapshot at the default 40% cut, a shock at the biggest outflow hubs (NVDA, SOL) reaches 18–19 of 51 markets in two or three waves, a shock at the S&P reaches only 3 in a single wave, and a shock at a peripheral market like agriculture (CORN) reaches no other market. The percolation line is the tipping point at which one shock spans ≥50% of the system — the cascade‑analysis read on systemic fragility. It is descriptive: it shows how the currently inferred structure would carry a shock, not whether one is coming.

Settings. Link strength is measured relative to the strongest asymmetry in the network: at the default 40% (slider 15–65%) only links with A(i→j) at least 40% of the largest A are used. Every positive asymmetry above the cut counts — the cascade does not apply the network's permutation gate. The source selector is grouped by factor basket and starts on the anticipation transmitter. The percolation line scans cuts from 90% down to 5% in 5-point steps and reports the highest one at which the chosen source still reaches ≥50% of markets (or "never spans ≥50%"). In Backtest, until the network has at least 12 shared days (about 2023-10-09; crypto history starts 2023-09-21), the panel says "not enough shared history at this date" instead of simulating.

Network structure — hubs, choke points & amplification

Read it: Three structural reads of the directed spillover network: where outflow concentrates (preferential-attachment hubs), which market is the bottleneck whose removal most fragments how a shock spreads (choke point), and how far a typical shock travels (amplifier vs suppressor).
Does NOT mean: NOT a forecast. The network is estimated and rewires; these are descriptive structure of the current inference. The choke point is not a "weak link that will break" — it is where the current inferred topology happens to bottleneck.

This panel is the honest residue of two more cross-domain analogies — metabolic pathways and evolutionary/social graph theory. Preferential attachment (the rich-get-richer power law seen in wealth and in how well-connected mutant genes take over a structured population) shows up as spillover concentration: a Gini and top-3 share of out-strength, and the hub list. Choke points are the metabolic-pathway idea of bottleneck reactions: the market whose removal most fragments shock propagation — which is genuinely distinct from the loudest transmitter (on the embedded snapshot, the top transmitter is NVDA while the top choke points are Canada (EWC) and EM local-currency sovereign debt (EMLC)). Amplification is evolutionary graph theory's amplifier-vs-suppressor distinction: how far a single shock reaches on average. What deliberately did not make it: flux-balance analysis (the predictive core of metabolic modelling) needs a conserved, stoichiometric quantity — markets have none (their #2 missing ingredient); and fixation-probability / evolutionary-dynamics models need an observable population of agents or strategies, which price data does not contain. Importing either would manufacture prediction the substrate can't support — so only the descriptive network structure transfers.

How it is computed. A market's out-strength is the sum of its positive asymmetries; the panel shows the top-3 share of all outflow, the Gini of out-strength and the five biggest hubs. Choke points: each of the 12 strongest hubs is removed in turn and the console measures how much total shock reach falls; the top four are listed. Amplification is the average share of markets a single shock reaches (≥40% = amplifier, ≥20% = mild amplifier, otherwise suppressor). Both use a fixed 40% link-strength cut, independent of the cascade slider. In Backtest, until the network has at least 12 shared days (about 2023-10-09; crypto history starts 2023-09-21), the panel says "not enough shared history at this date" instead of printing a Gini, hubs or a reach (an empty network would only give meaningless numbers).

Systemic coupling — spectral radius ρ̂ & absorption ratio

Read it: The 'Systemic coupling' panel shows two sparklines and current values: ρ̂ (spectral radius) and AR (absorption ratio). Both are plotted over time since 2016; the caveats explain they rise DURING crises (2020 and 2022 in the sparklines; 2008 in the wider literature), not before.
Does NOT mean: These are NOT early-warning signals of a crash. They are 'thermometer' readings of how unified the market currently is, analogous to critical-slowing-down in physics, but the analogy breaks in markets because the topology is estimated post-hoc, not fixed as in water systems.

These two metrics measure how tightly the whole cross-asset system is moving as a single unified entity. Both are coincident-to-lagging indicators: they spike during stress, not before.

Spectral radius ρ̂: The dominant eigenvalue (λ_max) of the rolling cross-asset correlation matrix, divided by the number of assets. Think of it as a proxy for how many independent modes of variation remain in the system. High ρ̂ (closer to 1) means the assets are "mode-collapsing" — correlations rising, individual shock absorption falling.

  • Formula: ρ̂ = λ_max / N (where N is the asset count).
  • This is an analogy to the VAR spectral radius ρ(Φ) from your seismic/hydraulic work, not a fitted dynamical operator.
  • The basis is the 48 non-crypto names (US and international equities, sectors, rates, credit, commodities, the dollar, FX and VIX); crypto is excluded so the history reaches back to 2016.
  • Current values use the last 252 trading days all 48 share; the sparklines are rolling 60-day windows stepped every 7 days across the whole history.

Absorption ratio (AR): The fraction of total variance explained by the top k eigenmodes, with k = N/5 = 10 for the 48 names. High AR = little local shock absorption; most of the move is concentrated in the leading modes. Kritzman-Li (2011) framed this as a fragility measure, but it too rises during crises, not before. The standardized AR-shift δ compares the mean of the three latest rolling AR values with the mean and spread of all earlier ones (a weak regime flag only). The panel also lists the eight largest loadings on the dominant mode — what drives the common factor. Cards that rank today's absorption against its own history (the crash badge, the diversification card and the perspectives) use a separate rolling history of the same 252-day measure, sampled on a fixed grid every 21 trading days from the start of the history, plus the current window, not the 60-day sparkline: the shorter windows read about 3 points higher on average, from sampling noise.

Why they are not leading indicators: Both metrics are fixed-topology readouts of realized correlation. When liquidity evaporates or systematic factors dominate (as in March 2020 or September 2022), all correlations spike simultaneously — the topology doesn't exist until after the move. The console's transparency: "ρ̂ is an analogy to the water system's fixed topology — except here the topology is estimated and rewires in a crisis."

What they tell you: High ρ̂/AR indicates the current market is moving in lock-step (regime of tight coupling), not that a break is imminent. A persistent high reading can last months or years with nothing happening; declines also start from low readings (base-rate honesty).

Game-theoretic lens — beauty contest & coordination risk

Read it: Two things. First, the strategic reason short-term direction can't be forecast (Keynes' beauty contest / higher-order beliefs). Second, a coordination/run-vulnerability gauge built from existing readings — strategic complementarity (coupling), crowding (share of markets already stretched), and whether the system's leader (focal point) is stable.
Does NOT mean: NOT a prediction that a run or crash will occur. The run-vulnerability gauge is a global-games framing that reads conditions off the cross-asset state — it never claims a coordinated move is imminent.

This makes the game theory that was implicit in the console explicit. Why direction is a guessing game: a price is the market's average guess about everyone else's average guess (Keynes' beauty contest); acting on a public signal moves the very price you are predicting (reflexivity); and any stable edge is competed toward zero. That higher-order-belief loop is the game-theoretic reason short-term direction is a martingale — the console's whole thesis, restated in game theory. Coordination / run-vulnerability: a crisis is a coordination failure (a run, a stampede, a currency attack). Global-games theory (Morris–Shin) says such failures get likelier when actions strongly reinforce each other (high complementarity ≈ high coupling), many players already sit near their tipping point (crowding), and the focal point is shifting (an unstable system leader). The gauge blends those three into a descriptive vulnerability read — context for the regime, not a timing signal.

The blend: 0–100 = 50% × coupling percentile + 30% × share of markets with fragility ≥60 + 20% if the anticipation transmitter is unstable across bootstrap resamples. Below 40 reads low, 40–65 moderate, 66 and above elevated.

Financial turbulence (Mahalanobis)

Read it: Read the turbulence value and its χ² percentile, then the list of today's biggest contributors. Treat the percentile as ordinal (fat tails make it optimistic). The shrinkage slider changes how much the covariance is pulled toward a scaled identity (equal variances, zero correlations).
Does NOT mean: Not a forecast of volatility or crashes. Not a threshold-crossing predictor—high turbulence can persist for days with no event, or spike on a single outlier. Not independent of spectral radius; both are multivariate stress measures that correlate during crises but neither causes the other. χ² tails are understated in financial returns.

Core Measure

The Mahalanobis distance of today's one-day cross-asset return vector (the 48 non-crypto names) from the mean of the last 252 shared trading days, scaled by that window's covariance. A multivariate extension of the per-asset surprise lens, capturing how far the system sits from its typical state in metric-adjusted space.

Calculation

  • Estimate mean μ and covariance Σ from the last 252 trading days all 48 names share (at least 3 × 48 = 144 are needed).
  • Shrink: Σα = (1−α)Σ + α·(tr Σ / N)·I — a fixed linear shrinkage toward equal variances and zero correlation (not Ledoit-Wolf's estimated intensity). α is the slider (0–0.8, default 0.20); if the matrix still cannot be inverted, the console raises α to at least 0.5 and says so.
  • Compute d = (x − μ)ᵀ Σα⁻¹ (x − μ) for today's one-day return vector x (and for every day in the window, for the sparkline).
  • Map today's d to a χ² CDF percentile using N = 48 degrees of freedom.
  • The sparkline is the 20-day rolling average of d across the window.

Interpretation

Higher turbulence = larger deviation from the system's covariance-weighted center. Rising turbulence during 2020 and 2022 crises is well-documented; it is a coincident-to-slightly-lagging read on regime stress, not a leading signal.

The drivers list shows each asset's contribution to today's Mahalanobis distance: xᵢ × (Σ⁻¹ x)ᵢ, ranked by size (top eight) — the assets pushing the system away from its center.

Caveat: Fat Tails & χ² Optimism

χ² percentiles are optimistic in financial fat tails—the true tail probability is worse. Treat the percentile as ordinal (higher = worse), not as a precise exceedance probability. During high turbulence, the true risk is underestimated.

Display

A line reading "Turbulence d (today) · χ²(48) percentile", the 20-day-smoothed sparkline, then today's top eight contributors. In Backtest everything is computed as of the cursor date.

Structural geography — market-state manifold (diffusion map)

Read it: Each dot is a 10-day cross-asset state; ψ₁ (left→right) is the stress axis. Find the ● now marker and the ◌ closest analog. A resemblance in geometry says nothing about what follows.
Does NOT mean: Not a predictor of next-regime transitions. Not a ranking of crisis severity—the manifold is a state portrait, not a potential-energy landscape. Nearest neighbors in the 10-day state space share historical return patterns, but financial systems do not repeat exactly. The manifold changes as new data arrive; past orbits are not predictive of future ones.

What It Shows

A nonlinear generalization of the coupling panel: every dot is a 10-day cross-asset state (the average standardized return of each macro name over 10 days), placed by a diffusion map so geometrically similar regimes sit together. Calm states cluster tightly; crises (2020, 2022) form a stressed arm. Current position and its nearest historical analog are highlighted.

Diffusion Map Construction

  • Daily returns of the 48 non-crypto names over all shared history, each z-scored over the full sample.
  • About 400 sampled days (428 on the embedded snapshot). Each state is the 48-number vector of mean z-returns over the previous 10 days; its "stress" is the average absolute z across those 10 days and 48 names.
  • A dense Gaussian kernel exp(−‖xᵢ − xⱼ‖² / ε) over all pairs of states, with ε = the median squared distance (no nearest-neighbour graph).
  • Symmetric degree normalisation, then the top three eigenvectors; ψ₁ and ψ₂ are the second and third (the first is trivial), scaled by their eigenvalues, with ψ₁'s sign set so that higher = more stress.
  • Dots are coloured by their stress relative to the most stressed state (green calm → amber → red stressed arm).

Interpretation

Proximity = geometric resemblance in the realized cross-asset state. Where today sits—and which past state it most resembles—is a coincident read on the regime, never a forecast. The manifold structure is a nonlinear generalization of PCA/spectral structure, showing how the system inhabits its state space.

Honest Caveat

⚠ COINCIDENT STATE GEOMETRY, NOT A FORECAST. A "closest analog" is a geometric resemblance—it does not imply the same outcome follows. Historical recurrence is rare in financial systems; initial conditions drift, and regimes change. The manifold is a coincident snapshot of structure, useful for regime classification, not for predicting the next move.

Backtest caveat: the map is built from the full history, and the analog search can pick a date after the cursor — so in Backtest this panel (and the briefing's analog sentence) has look-ahead.

Display

A static scatter in the first two diffusion coordinates (no hover or tooltips; on phones it scrolls sideways). ● marks the state nearest today (or the cursor); the dashed ◌ ring marks the closest analog — the state whose raw 10-day, 48-market vector is nearest by Euclidean distance, excluding states within 60 days of now. Fragility thermometer

Read it: One row per market: a 0–100 score with three mini-bars for its channels (persistence · amplitude · tail). Rows are grouped by factor basket by default; click "sort: basket ▾" to sort by score instead, and click a row to load that market into Indicator detail.
Does NOT mean: A high score is NOT a crash-prediction tool. It describes how fast the asset's stress signature has been changing over the last 60 days relative to its own history — moves getting larger, mean-reversion weakening, tails getting heavier: a trend, not a level. The console explicitly warns: 'DESCRIPTIVE, NOT PREDICTIVE… A resilience thermometer, not a crash detector… [it] can persist for months or years with nothing happening.'

The console's headline score: a composite 0–100 reading per asset, built from seven indicators grouped into three channels. They are not independent; they overlap significantly, especially the persistence measures.

How the score is built

  1. Seven indicators on a rolling 252-day window of daily log returns: lag-1 autocorrelation and the recovery rate λ = −ln φ (both on EWMA-volatility-standardized residuals), DFA / Hurst α (recomputed every 3 days), annualized realized volatility, the bimodality coefficient, skewness and excess kurtosis (see Indicator detail).
  2. Direction-adjust so that higher = more fragile (λ and skewness are sign-flipped).
  3. Trend, not level: take each indicator's 60-day OLS slope and turn it into an expanding z-score against that asset's own history of slopes.
  4. Three channels: persistence = mean of the AC1, λ and DFA z-scores; amplitude = mean of realized vol and bimodality; tail = mean of skewness and kurtosis.
  5. Composite = mean of the three channels, turned into an expanding z-score again and mapped to 0–100 with the normal CDF. The mini-bars are the three channels on the same 0–100 scale.

Score interpretation: A high reading (e.g., 70/100) means that asset's stress indicators — persistence, amplitude, tail — have been rising over the last 60 days more steeply than is usual for that asset. It is a descriptive summary of how the asset's own behaviour is changing, not a forecast. An asset that is already very volatile but no longer getting more so can score low.

Display: a basket-colour dot, the score bar (green below 40, light green 40–59, amber 60–84, red 85+), the value, and the three mini-bars. A score needs about a year of history (252 days) before it exists and a further 60 days before its trend term means anything, so crypto (history from September 2023) has a shorter record.

Behavior in the wild: High fragility readings can persist for months or years with nothing happening. Declines also routinely start from low readings, so a low score does not guarantee stability. The console is explicit: "A high reading can persist for months or years with nothing happening, and declines also start from low readings."

Honest caveats per channel:

  • Persistence (AC1, λ, DFA): AC1 is the weakest channel in markets; λ carries the same information as AC1; DFA needs long windows and lags the regime shift.
  • Amplitude (realized vol, bimodality): mostly restates known volatility clustering (GARCH); often coincident, not leading; apparent flickering has a high false-positive rate.
  • Tail (skewness, kurtosis): one extreme day flips or dominates the estimators; very noisy.

Why the critical-slowing-down analogy breaks: Physics systems (lasers, neurons) show genuine early warning via reduced variance and coherence before a bifurcation. Markets invert this: price is the expectation; there's no hidden state to telemeter. Correlations are estimated and rewire instantly on news, so no fixed topology exists until after the move. The console is unsparing: "The critical-slowing-down framing is an analogy from physics (these signals were largely absent before historical meltdowns); the volatility channel mostly restates GARCH."

Each fragility score rests on seven indicators. They are not independent votes—persistence indicators especially overlap. Only the two autocorrelation indicators (AC1 and λ) are computed on EWMA(0.94) volatility-standardized residuals; the others use raw daily log returns. Display order: AC1, recovery rate, realized vol, bimodality, skewness, excess kurtosis, DFA.

Persistence (3 indicators, averaged)

  • Lag-1 autocorrelation (CSD) — vol-standardized to avoid measuring GARCH. Rising = slower recovery from shocks. Weakest channel in markets; hard to move the needle.
  • Recovery rate λ = −ln φ (φ = AR(1) parameter) — same information as AC1, repackaged. Falling λ = slower return to equilibrium. A sibling, not an independent vote.
  • DFA / Hurst α — rising toward 1 = more persistence / less mean-reversion. Needs long windows (252-day rolling, computed every 3 days); descriptive, often lags the regime shift by weeks.

Amplitude (2 indicators, averaged)

  • Realized volatility (annualized) — rising = larger fluctuations / higher stress. Mostly restates known volatility clustering (GARCH); often coincident, not leading. Computed as √(252 × variance of the 252-day window's daily returns).
  • Bimodality / flickering — rising = switching between calm and stressed states. Vol clustering alone produces apparent switching; high false-positive rate. Computed from skewness, kurtosis, and sample size.

Tail (2 indicators, averaged)

  • Skewness — more negative = asymmetry toward big drops. Returns are always left-skewed; one bad day flips the sign. Estimator is noisy.
  • Excess kurtosis — rising = fatter tails. Estimator dominated by 1–2 extreme days; very noisy. High variance, little signal between regimes in practice.

Surprise lens (active-inference analogue)

Read it: One row per market: a ~120-day sparkline of its 20-day-smoothed surprise, then a 0–100 number for how surprising its last ~20 days of moves were to a simple causal AR(1)+EWMA-volatility model, relative to its own history. High = recent moves keep beating that simple model's expectations — a coincident read, not a warning.
Does NOT mean: Not a leading indicator. Not volatility or variance—it is a residual innovation metric conditioned on a 60-day local model. Not a forecast of returns or crashes. Not independent of fragility—both rise during stress, but neither causes the other.

Core Concept

One-step negative log-likelihood (NLL) from a simple AR(1) + EWMA model. Analogue of variational free energy from active inference: higher surprise = larger deviation from the asset's local predictive model. Reported as a 0–100 reading: today's 20-day-smoothed NLL standardized against the mean and spread of all earlier smoothed values for that asset, mapped through the normal CDF.

Calculation

For each day t ≥ 1:

  • Fit the AR(1) coefficient φ on a rolling 60-day window ending at t−1.
  • Predict next day: μ = φ × r[t−1].
  • Compute NLL: 0.5 × ((r[t] − μ) / σ)² + log(σ), where σ = √v and v is a running EWMA variance (started from the first 60 returns).
  • Update variance via EWMA: v ← 0.94 × v + 0.06 × (r[t] − μ)².
  • Smooth with 20-day rolling average.
  • Convert to a 0–100 reading using the mean and std of the smoothed series up to that day (walk-forward).

Interpretation

High surprise (70–100) = asset behavior deviates far from its own simple local predictive model. Not a forecast signal, but a coincident read on how much the asset is surprising itself. During stress (2020, 2022), surprise tends to rise, along with vol and fragility.

Why "Active Inference"?

In active inference, the system minimizes surprise (prediction error) to maintain a generative model. Here, we invert it: surprise measures how much the asset's local model is failing. Rising surprise = environment is drifting away from the model, not that a crash is imminent.

Shock clustering — Hawkes self-excitation

Read it: Read the system branching ratio n (0 = shocks isolated, → 1 = each shock triggers about one more) and the shock half-life, then the per-asset list. High n means aftershocks are under way now — not that a new shock is coming.
Does NOT mean: Not a predictor of the next shock. Not a measure of systemic importance or contagion. High branching ratio = feedback loops are active, not that a crash is coming. n is very noisy with few events; a single bad day changes the fit significantly. Not independent of turbulence or fragility—all three rise together during crises, but none causes the others.

Core Concept

Do large moves beget more large moves? Fits a self-exciting point process to large return shocks (≥2.5σ magnitude, measured against each asset's EWMA volatility). The branching ratio n is how self-reinforcing the clustering is: n ≈ 0.3 means shocks decay; n ≈ 0.9 means critical (on the brink of runaway); n = 1 is unstable.

Calculation

  • For each of the 51 assets, standardize daily returns by an EWMA volatility (λ = 0.94) and mark days with |z| above 2.5 as shocks.
  • Pool all assets' shocks into one daily count over the 751 calendar days ending at the newest data day of any series (2026-10-21 on the embedded snapshot, a day past the 2026-10-20 markets as-of date; after a live refresh, the newest refreshed day), or ending at the cursor in Backtest. Weekends, and days on which a series has no data, count as zero-shock days.
  • Fit Hawkes process: λ(t) = μ + α Σ exp(−β(t − tᵢ)), where μ = background rate, α = jump size, β = decay rate — in discrete daily time, by maximizing a Poisson log-likelihood.
  • Grid search over (μ, α, β) — 6 × 6 × 5 values — then four rounds of local refinement.
  • Compute branching ratio: n = α exp(−β) / (1 − exp(−β)). This is the expected number of children per event in a Galton-Watson branching process.
  • Also report half-life: how fast aftershocks decay (log(2) / β, in calendar days).
  • Repeat the fit per asset for every asset with at least 8 shocks in the window.

Interpretation

CONTEMPORANEOUS: high n = shocks CURRENTLY clustering (aftershocks underway), NOT a coming exogenous mainshock. Rising branching ratio during stress (2022) means the cascade is self-sustaining. However, a high n does not forecast the next shock's magnitude or timing—only that if a shock occurs, more are likely to follow quickly. It is the temporal twin of ρ(Φ) → 1 (cf. the induced-seismicity pilot).

Caveats

  • Least-stable panel: single exponential kernel, daily bins, often few jump events per asset (per-asset fits need ≥8). Confidence in n is low.
  • Threshold-dependent: moving 2.5σ to 3.0σ or 2.0σ (the console fixes it at 2.5) changes the count and fit.
  • Temporal resolution: daily bins may miss intra-day clustering and aftershock sequences that resolve in hours.

Display

The system branching ratio n with a 0–100 bar (clipped at 1.0) and a sparkline of the fitted intensity, the shock half-life, the total shock count, then bars for the eight assets with the highest per-asset n. A message appears if there are too few shocks (five or fewer in total) to fit a reliable process.

Anticipation — where stress starts, & the out-of-sample test (the predictive layer)

Read it: This panel is the companion paper's actual engine made live. It names the upstream transmitter (the market whose stress tends to lead the others — where to direct attention, not a trade), contrasts it with the loudest (most volatile) market, and runs a genuine out-of-sample test of whether the transmitter actually leads.
Does NOT mean: A buy/sell signal or a price/return forecast. Any out-of-sample lead it finds is small in absolute terms — it sharpens where stress originates, not whether to trade.

Every other panel is descriptive — it tells you the state now. This one is the closest thing to a predictive reading the evidence honestly supports, and it comes straight from the companion research paper ("Directedness, not connectedness, decides when to anticipate a contagion").

What it computes

  • Net transmitter (Diebold–Yılmaz): a one-lag vector autoregression (lightly ridge-regularized) on the standardized absolute daily returns of the 48 long-history macro names, decomposed with a generalized forecast-error-variance decomposition over 8 days into directed volatility spillovers. The market that exports more stress than it imports (highest NET) is the transmitter — the "anticipation target": the place to watch, for attention, not for trades.
  • Loudest market: the basis series with the highest mean absolute daily log change over the full sample — the most volatile of the 48, the instinctive "react here" target, which is usually downstream. For the out-of-sample test it is re-picked on the training split. Because VIX is a volatility index whose log changes dwarf those of every price series, it wins "loudest" by construction, whatever the data; when the loudest is VIX the card says the transmitter-vs-loudest comparison is weak, since it is then benchmarked against a fixed choice rather than a data-driven one.
  • Total directed spillover (Diebold–Yılmaz connectedness, %) and directedness (0–1): how one-way the pairwise spillovers are. The paper's core claim: anticipation can only help when the network is directionally asymmetric — when the transmitter and the loudest market differ. The verdict line: if the transmitter is the loudest, reacting is as good as anticipating; if total spillover is below 35% or directedness below 0.12, there is no clear upstream source; otherwise the transmitter is the top net exporter of spillover in this full-sample decomposition — a place to direct attention, not a trade — and whether that buys any real lead is left to the out-of-sample test.
  • Transmitter stability: 100 block-bootstrap refits report how often the same market comes out on top and a 95% interval for its rank, plus a 95% bootstrap interval for its NET. If it tops fewer than half the resamples or its rank interval reaches past 3, the leadership counts as unstable: on the panel the stability row turns amber (green when stable), and the plain-English briefing and the game-theoretic lens's focal-point row say in words that it is unstable.

The out-of-sample test (why this is honest, not hype)

The engine splits the shared history into a training part (first 60%) and a held-out test part (last 40%). It picks the transmitter and the loudest market on the training part only, then checks on the unseen test part how well each one's stress today correlates with the whole basket's average stress five days later. It reports both correlations, the transmitter's advantage, and the share of 200 block-bootstrap resamples of the test period in which that advantage is positive. It also reports two fairer baselines on the same test: the basket's own 5-day stress persistence (basket stress today against basket stress five days later, with its own count of resamples in which the transmitter beats it), and the transmitter's rank among all 48 basis markets. Because VIX wins "loudest" by construction, beating it says little: on the embedded snapshot SPY's advantage over VIX is positive in 100% of resamples, yet SPY (0.149) ranks 3rd of 48 (median 0.08) and does not beat persistence (0.151; ahead in 48% of resamples).

The verdict is keyed to the persistence baseline, not to the loudest market, and has three branches: a lead over the loudest that also beats the basket's own persistence in at least 90% of resamples → "a small out-of-sample lead that also beats the basket's own 5-day stress persistence" (still small in absolute terms); a lead over the loudest only → the card gives the transmitter's rank and the persistence comparison and says this test cannot tell transmitter leadership apart from ordinary volatility clustering (the case on the 2026-10-20 snapshot, in line with the companion paper's own real-data result: positive but not statistically significant); otherwise → no out-of-sample lead on this split. When the transmitter's correlation and the baseline round to the same two decimals, the card shows a third. Either way it sharpens where stress tends to originate — exactly why the panel refuses to call it a trade signal. Lead is association, not causation, and on non-stationary data the transmitter migrates over time — read the stability rows.

The panel also carries a methods caveat: variance spillover can distort linear Granger-type inference (Mantalos & Shukur 2010); this is a forecast-error-variance decomposition, not impulse-response causality, and a nonlinear-Granger check (Wismüller et al. 2021) is the robustness alternative. The engine runs on the full history — it does not follow the Backtest cursor.

Alpha Lab — can you actually trade the signal?

Read it: A walk-forward backtest that tests, honestly, whether the stress signal makes money — net of trading costs, benchmarked against simply buying and holding SPY. A collapsed card on the Predictive tab: open it, pick a stance (Risk-off / Contrarian / UUP/SPY), the entry threshold τ, a cost in basis points, a holding period and a position size.
Does NOT mean: Sold alpha or a live trading signal. It is research. The likely honest result — confirmed in the build — is that the edge bleeds out after friction and underperforms buy-and-hold.

This panel answers the natural question — "there's a tiny statistical lead, can I monetise it?" — the only honest way: by simulating it. Your chosen stance supplies the direction the data cannot.

How it trades (in simulation)

  • Signal: walk-forward turbulence — each day's Mahalanobis distance of the 48-name return vector from the previous 252 days (covariance refreshed every 10 days, shrinkage 0.2). The entry threshold τ is a percentile of the training split (the first 60% of days), so there is no look-ahead; slider 50th–90th, default 60th. The Contrarian stance always uses the 90th percentile, whatever the slider says. That holds in the simulation and for the configuration the rigorous validation below scores. The validation's 15-trial sweep still runs Contrarian at all five τ, and those runs count toward its no-skill bar and PBO.
  • Stances: Risk-off = short ½ SPY and ½ XLF, long ½ TLT and ½ UUP; Contrarian = the reverse; UUP/SPY = long UUP, short SPY.
  • Entry and exit: enter when turbulence crosses τ; exit after the holding period (slider 3–10 days, default 5) or when turbulence falls below 0.8τ. For Risk-off and UUP/SPY a regime filter stands aside — or exits — while the 60-day SPY–TLT correlation is above 0.2 (the flight-to-quality premise has broken, as in 2022), though it detects that late.
  • Costs and size: a cost in basis points (slider 2–10, default 4) charged on the gross position at both entry and exit; position size 0.25–1.0× (default 0.25×, margin-of-safety). The walk-forward box (on by default) makes TEST the period after the training split; unticked, TEST covers the whole history.

What it reports

A headline net TEST result next to SPY buy-and-hold total return over the same dates, an equity-curve chart (net strategy, gross, SPY), and a table of net Sharpe, total return, max drawdown and trade count for TEST and TRAIN, plus win rate on active days and cost as a share of gross PnL for TEST only (their TRAIN cells are left blank). The TRAIN column is flagged "⚠overfit" when its Sharpe beats TEST by more than 0.5. The result is stamped INCONCLUSIVE when the test Sharpe is below 0.1, the net return is not positive, there are fewer than 20 trades, or the Deflated Sharpe from the rigorous validation below is under 50%. The last rule means a positive net number never appears unlabelled when the console's own overfitting check says it does not clear the no-skill bar; the caveat then names the net return and the Deflated Sharpe side by side.

What it shows, as built: on the embedded snapshot at the default settings, all three stances come out INCONCLUSIVE and far behind SPY buy-and-hold (+95.2% over the same test dates) once friction is charged. Contrarian is the closest call: net +1.2% on test, but its Deflated Sharpe is 9%, so it does not clear what 15 no-skill tries would produce. That is the point. It is the computational form of Bogle's lesson: the small volatility-correlation the Anticipation card measures out of sample — a few percent of variance; the panel quotes it live from that card rather than as a fixed number — is the kind of edge frictions quietly consume. Use it to disprove trade ideas cheaply, not to find a money machine.

Rigorous validation (the "better cross-validation" answer). The validation runs automatically with every backtest (the button only recomputes it). It sweeps 15 configurations (3 stances × τ at the 50th, 60th, 70th, 80th and 90th percentiles, at your current cost, holding period and size) and reports two overfitting diagnostics: a Deflated Sharpe Ratio (Bailey–López de Prado — the probability that the scored configuration's Sharpe beats the best you'd expect from 15 no-skill tries, adjusted for skew and fat tails; the scored configuration is the grid point nearest your settings — your stance at the swept τ closest to the slider, always the 90th percentile for Contrarian — and the panel names it) and the Probability of Backtest Overfitting via CSCV (combinatorially symmetric cross-validation: the history is cut into 10 equal blocks and every 5-versus-5 split is tried; PBO is how often the configuration that looked best in-sample lands below the median out-of-sample). There is no purging or embargo between the blocks. The panel labels PBO of 50% or more "likely overfit", 30–50% "fragile", below 30% "is not obviously overfit" — and whenever the Deflated Sharpe is under 50% it adds that no configuration clears the no-skill bar, so a low PBO never reads as an edge. Both numbers move with the sliders, so apart from the default-settings Deflated Sharpe quoted above, this guide does not quote them. These tests shrink apparent edge; they never create it.

The constructive flip side — predicting volatility instead of direction — has its own card: see Forward-volatility lab. Like it, the Alpha Lab is computed from the history at load: it does not follow the Backtest cursor and is not redone after a live refresh until you reload.

Forward-volatility lab — predict what's predictable

Read it: Predictive tab. Rows: SPY's realized volatility over the next 5 and 20 days predicted from simple price-state features (out-of-sample R²), forward direction as a control (scored on the same three features), the same model with forward-macro features added, and a no-model persistence baseline. Higher R² = more of the next period's volatility explained; at or below 0 = no better than predicting the average.
Does NOT mean: NOT a price or direction forecast — direction is the control row and stays at about zero (at or below zero, or within ±0.02). A learnable volatility forecast is risk context, never a signal to trade or hedge — the ridge model is not wired to the Risk tab's sizing dial, which applies only your own band to SPY's trailing 20-day volatility — and an R² of 0.2–0.4 still leaves most of the next period's volatility unexplained.

The constructive complement to the Alpha Lab: point the same honest machinery at what is learnable. A ridge regression (λ = 1) on three risk-state features — SPY's trailing 20-day realized volatility, the Alpha Lab's walk-forward turbulence, and the 60-day SPY–TLT correlation — is scored with blocked 5-fold cross-validation, dropping 20 days of training data on either side of each test fold (purge + embargo) so overlapping windows cannot leak.

On the embedded snapshot (markets as of 2026-10-20): forward 5-day volatility R² ≈ 0.41 and forward 20-day volatility R² ≈ 0.22, while forward direction, scored as a control on the same three features, comes out at ≈0 at both horizons (at or below zero, or within ±0.02) — no better than simply predicting the average. Today's volatility used directly as the forecast (the persistence baseline) scores −0.09; that naive baseline, not the ridge model, is what the Risk tab's sizing dial uses. The 5-day reads higher than the 20-day because volatility clustering is strongest at short lags — it does not make the model "more predictive" of anything else. That contrast is the whole lesson: aim any learning at vol/regime/risk, not at price direction.

The "more data" experiment — an honest negative. A natural instinct is "add the best forward-content macro data and the model improves." The lab tests exactly that: it adds the 10y−2y yield curve, the high-yield spread, the Chicago Fed financial-conditions index, VIX and the 10-year real rate as features. The out-of-sample result is that they do not help the 20-day model — they slightly degrade it (R² 0.22 → 0.17). This is reported on the panel with a red ▼0.05 (computed before rounding, so it can differ by 0.01 from the gap between the two rounded figures), not hidden. It is the "shallow wins" / overfitting lesson demonstrated on live data: more and better data does not equal better prediction — slow, correlated features add parameters that overfit even under ridge regularization. Those macro series carry genuine signal, but at the recession / regime horizon (months), which is why they live in the Forward-macro panel — not inside a 20-day model.

The lab is computed once from the history at load: it does not follow the Backtest cursor and is not redone after a live refresh until you reload. Its 5- and 20-day results also feed the meta-learning layer's skill board.

Forecast reliability — the meta-learning layer

Read it: A layer above the forecasts. It does not predict — it reports when each forecast can be trusted: a skill board (out-of-sample R² for 5- and 20-day volatility vs direction) and a regime-conditional reliability table (how forecastable volatility is in the low-vol / mid-vol / high-vol terciles of SPY's 20-day volatility), with the tercile SPY's latest 20-day vol falls in marked.
Does NOT mean: NOT a way to manufacture directional alpha. Conditioning on regime improves your sense of how much to trust the volatility/risk forecast — it is not an input to exposure, and it never makes direction forecastable. Direction stays R²≈0 in every regime.

This is meta-labeling in its only honest form (after López de Prado): know when the working risk model is more or less reliable — not to change exposure, and not to invent a directional signal. The skill board repeats the forward-volatility lab's out-of-sample R². The regime table splits SPY's history into terciles of trailing 20-day volatility (low-vol / mid-vol / high-vol, about 857 days each) and, within each, reports how well trailing 5-day volatility tracks the next 5 days' volatility, and trailing 5-day return the next 5 days' return (squared correlations — in-sample, unlike the skill board).

The regime table makes two facts concrete. First, volatility is far more forecastable when stress is already elevated — its regime-conditional R² climbs from roughly 0.01 in the low-vol tercile to ~0.08 mid-vol to ~0.36 high-vol (clustering is strongest in turmoil). Second, direction is unforecastable in every regime (R²≈0 throughout). So the honest reading is descriptive: the risk reading is more informative in high-vol stretches than in calm ones. It is not an input to exposure — the only sizing mechanism on the console is your own pre-set, down-only band on SPY's trailing 20-day volatility, which takes no regime input — and never a direction call or a reason to trade. The panel marks the tercile SPY's latest 20-day vol falls in, as of SPY's last data day (not the Backtest cursor), with the value and the tercile boundaries: on the embedded snapshot, 8.8% annualised as of 2026-10-21, in the Low-vol tercile (boundaries 10.1% and 15.2%) — a vol tercile, not a stress alarm. It is computed once at load from SPY's full history.

Overnight vs intraday — the one free directional regularity

Read it: Pick a fund (SPY, QQQ, IWM, GLD or TLT) and a window (full history or the last 3 years). The card splits each day into its overnight (close→open) and intraday (open→close) halves, plotting the cumulative return of holding only-overnight vs only-intraday vs buy-and-hold, with annualised figures. Most of the equity-index return has historically accrued overnight.
Does NOT mean: NOT a tradable edge. You cannot capture the close-to-open gap net of spreads, slippage and overnight-gap risk. It is a risk premium / opening-auction print artifact, not informational alpha, and not a forecast.

This card is the honest answer to "what free public data predicts short-term direction?" The answer is: almost nothing — except this one documented, index-level regularity (Lou-Polk-Skouras 2019; the New York Fed's overnight-drift work). On this data SPY's overnight half earns about +9.0%/yr versus +4.1%/yr intraday — roughly two-thirds of the buy-and-hold return — and small-caps (IWM) are starker still: the intraday half is actually negative, so effectively all of the return came overnight.

Why it is not a trade. The drift has persisted in this sample rather than arbitraging away — which is precisely the signature of a risk premium (you are paid for bearing overnight-gap and dealer-inventory / liquidity-provision risk) plus an opening-auction print artifact, not free informational alpha. You cannot isolate the gap without paying spreads and slippage that erase it, and you would still carry the overnight-gap risk you are being compensated for. Switch the selector to TLT (Treasuries) and the pattern inverts (about −6.3%/yr overnight vs +1.7%/yr intraday over the full history; buy-and-hold lost money there, so the card says which half carried the loss instead of quoting a share of the return), which suggests an equity-flow effect rather than a universal law — though GLD (gold) shows the equity-like pattern too (about +13.5%/yr overnight vs −1.5%/yr intraday), so the split is not purely about equities.

It is the closest free data gets to short-term directional structure, included precisely to show why that structure is still not a forecast you can trade — the same lesson as the Alpha Lab and the volatility lab, made concrete on a real, named anomaly. The card uses its own embedded daily open/close history for these five funds (June 2016 – October 2026), which is never refreshed live; "Last 3 years" means the last 756 sessions.

Long-horizon valuation thermometer

Read it: Predictive tab. Where the US market's Shiller CAPE sits versus its own history: the latest embedded value and its percentile since 1881 and since 1950, the multiple of the long-run median, the earnings yield next to the 10-year Treasury yield (a rough equity risk premium), and the 10-year real return a simple CAPE regression implies, with a 95% band.
Does NOT mean: NOT a timing signal and NOT a forecast you can act on this year. The one place the academic canon (Cochrane's discount-rate result; Shiller's CAPE) says market direction is even weakly forecastable from free data — and only over many years. Cross-sectional factor demo — what "doing what quants do" returns

Read it: Predictive tab, amber border. A frozen exhibit — the result of an offline backtest, not computed in the page — of the one quant playbook free data allows: rank 85 large-cap stocks each month on a no-fit blend of three documented factors (12-minus-1-month momentum, 1-month reversal, low volatility) and go long the top fifth, short the bottom fifth. Read it top to bottom: is the signal there, does it pay after costs, does it persist, does breadth rescue it.
Does NOT mean: NOT a strategy, NOT a signal and NOT a recommendation — the finding is that there is no investable edge. The numbers are fixed; they do not update with the console's data.
  • Is the signal there? Composite rank information coefficient +0.021 (next month, out-of-sample), 95% interval −0.021 to +0.062, p = 0.33 — indistinguishable from zero; t = 1.0 against the field's discovery hurdle of t > 3.0 (Harvey–Liu–Zhu). Per signal: momentum +0.029, reversal +0.045, low-vol −0.041 (suspect).
  • Does it pay? Gross +4.6%/yr (Sharpe 0.26) shrinks to +3.2%/yr at 5 bps per side, +1.8% at 10, −1.1% at 20 and −3.9% at 30. Break-even is about 16 bps per side — below an honest all-in cost (20–30 bps of spread and impact plus about 50 bps a year to borrow the shorts). Net max drawdown −37.6%.
  • Does it persist? IC +0.042 in 2016–21 → about 0 in 2021–26 — though the decay itself is not significant, so "real edge that faded" cannot be told apart from noise.
  • Does breadth rescue it? Widening the same model from 85 hand-picked mega-caps to 589 names takes the IC to −0.003 and the gross Sharpe to −0.06 (break-even −3 bps: a loss even trading free). Breadth amplifies real signal; here there was none to amplify — Grinold's law run in reverse.

Survivorship, stated on the card: the universe is 85 current (2026-picked) large-caps, so failed and delisted names are missing. That inflates momentum and reversal and may flip low-vol's sign — the true effect is at most what is shown — while picking liquid mega-caps deflates the real premia, which live in small, illiquid names. The number is squeezed from both sides.

What it proves is epistemic: the accessible levers are honesty instruments that cleanly reject a fake edge, not machines that manufacture a real one. Professionals make a similarly tiny IC pay with what free data cannot buy — a delisting-inclusive universe, microcap access, a securities-lending desk, and execution that keeps turnover below the cost cliff.

It is FRED series RECPROUSM156N, the Chauvet–Piger smoothed U.S. recession probability — a Markov-switching model on coincident indicators (payrolls, industrial production, real income, real sales) that estimates whether the economy is in recession now, published with a lag. It is not a yield-curve model and not a 12-month-ahead probability: in the embedded data it reads ~99% during 2008–09 and near 100% in spring 2020, yet it stayed below 3% through the 2022–24 curve inversion. It confirms a recession; it does not forecast one. High-yield spreads and the financial-conditions index lead equity stress by weeks to months. The honest division of labour with the vol lab: short-horizon risk (vol, days-to-weeks) is best read from price state; medium-horizon regime (recession, months) is best read here. Forcing the months-horizon data into a days-horizon model overfits (see the vol-lab experiment), so the console keeps them separate.

How to read it: the card's read says Elevated recession / financial-stress risk when the recession probability is above 20%, the 10y−2y curve is inverted, financial conditions are above 0.5, or the high-yield spread is above 6%; Some forward stress building when the probability is above 5%, the spread above 4.5% or conditions above 0; otherwise Forward macro benign (curve positive, credit tight, conditions loose, recession probability low). Historically the curve inverting, the HY spread widening through ~4.5–6%, and financial conditions turning positive (tight) have been the meaningful regime shifts — context on a months clock, not a trigger for anything.

The series are an embedded FRED snapshot retrieved 23 October 2026 (daily sampled points 2026-10-15–2026-10-22; weekly latest points 2026-10-16–2026-10-17; monthly latest points 2026-07–2026-09), never refreshed live; each sparkline is the last ~120 observations, so how much time it spans depends on how often the series is sampled: about 10 years for the monthly rows (recession probability, unemployment), a little over 2 years for the weekly ones (both curve spreads, financial conditions, jobless claims) and only about 5.5 months for the daily high-yield spread. The high-yield history itself starts in June 2023, so in Backtest its row is absent before 19 June 2023 and its sparkline has fewer points until December 2023. The card follows the Backtest cursor.

Behavioral guardrail — brake yourself, not the market

Read it: Write if-then pre-commitments (e.g. "if about to sell after a >10% drop, wait 24h"), log each buy or sell, and watch two counters: decisions in the last 30 / 7 days (overtrading) and sells made after a drop (panic-prone). Does NOT mean: NOT a trade signal and NOT proof the journal works — the evidence is about investor behaviour in general; this journal itself is untested. It never blocks you; the friction is a pause. And it is walled off from every market gauge: no reading on this console may justify a trade.

The one lever the evidence says matters most for outcomes is behaviour, not the market. The card audits yours against the strongest retail evidence (Barber–Odean: overtrading hurts; panic-sellers who exit rarely buy back). Volatility-sizing dial — a down-only throttle

Read it: Set your comfort band (target volatility, 5–40%, default 15%) and read the exposure your own rule implies: min(100%, your target ÷ SPY's trailing 20-day volatility, annualised — the naive forecast it uses), floored at 30%. Below it, a walk-forward check shows what that rule did to SPY's drawdowns, and what it cost in turnover.
Does NOT mean: NOT a direction call and NOT a buy/sell signal. It is your band applied to SPY's trailing volatility used as a naive forecast — it only ever suggests holding less, never levers up, and earns no return. No Sharpe ratio is shown because the out-of-sample benefit of volatility targeting is fragile (Cederburg et al.).

How it works. The "forecast" is simply SPY's trailing 20-day realized volatility, annualized, carried forward unchanged — the naive persistence forecast that the forward-volatility lab uses as its no-model baseline (used raw as a level forecast it scores R²≈−0.09 there: worse than predicting the average, because volatility mean-reverts and the raw level overshoots after spikes — though the lab's fitted model gets much of its skill from this same input; a read on risk, never on direction), not the lab's learnable ridge model, which is not wired to the dial. The card labels it "Trailing 20-day vol (annualised) — the naive forecast it uses". Exposure = min(100%, your target ÷ that volatility), never below 30%: when it is above your band the rule says hold less; when it is below, the rule says 100% — never more. You set the band; you decide whether to follow the rule.

Walk-forward check. Over SPY's full embedded history the card applies the rule day by day, each day's exposure using only earlier returns, and compares the maximum drawdown of buy-and-hold with that of the vol-sized version, plus the turnover the rule generates (exposure changes per year). On the embedded snapshot at the default 15% band, buy-and-hold's maximum drawdown is −34% against −20% vol-sized, at a sizing turnover of 2.3× a year: down-sizing has shallowed drawdowns on this history — but it cannot earn return, and a twitchier band trades more.

The rule is about risk you have already decided to carry, written down in advance — exactly the kind of pre-commitment the guardrail asks for.

Crash-context badge — the regime you're already in

Read it: Four tail readings side by side — turbulence (0–100), the absorption ratio (labelled "Absorption (top-10-mode share)": the variance share of the top 10 eigenmodes, not a one-factor share), Hawkes shock clustering n, and SPY's 60-day return skew — and one phrase: Benign tail regime (none flagged), Mixed — some tail signals up (one or two), or Elevated left-tail regime (three or four), followed by the date it describes: "now" when the data is fresh, "as of YYYY-MM-DD (… · N days old)" when the data is more than 4 days old (the oldest series end; on the embedded snapshot that is crypto's 2026-10-20, while turbulence and absorption (the 48 non-crypto names), the skew (SPY) and the Hawkes n (fitted on shocks through the newest bar of any series) all run through 2026-10-21, a day later — see Hawkes), and the cursor date in Backtest (where it notes that the skew still uses the latest data). A reading is flagged at turbulence ≥70, absorption (its current 252-day value) in the top 20% of the rolling history of that same 252-day measure (sampled on a fixed grid every 21 trading days from the start of the history, plus the current window; with fewer than 12 samples, at a very early Backtest cursor, no percentile is shown and it is not flagged), Hawkes n ≥0.6, or skew ≤ −0.3. Absorption is judged against the history of the same 252-day measure, not a fixed level and not the 60-day sparkline (shorter windows read systematically higher): for these 48 names the 252-day level has stayed between about 76% and 91% since mid-2017, so any fixed cut would fire every day and the badge could never read Benign.
Does NOT mean: NOT a forecast and NOT a crash warning. Every one of these rises during 2020/2022, not before, and skew flips on a single bad day. The badge is wired to nothing — it moves no dial and predicts nothing.

A pure annotation of the tail regime you are already in. Three of its inputs come from other panels (turbulence, the absorption ratio — compared with a rolling history of the same 252-day absorption measure, not the panel's 60-day sparkline, which runs about 3 points higher on average — and the Hawkes branching ratio); the fourth is the skewness of SPY's last 60 daily returns. It is coincident by construction: you read it; you decide, by rules you set beforehand.

Diversification reality check

Read it: How many independent bets an equal-weighted basket of the 48 non-crypto markets really holds: the participation ratio (effective number of factors, from the factor-baskets panel), the diversification ratio (average volatility ÷ portfolio volatility; higher = more diversified), the top three names' share of total risk, and the four largest risk contributors.
Does NOT mean: NOT a promise that diversification will hold. The diversification ratio, top-3 share and risk contributions describe only the last two years of correlations; in a crisis correlations rush toward one and the effective number of bets collapses further. NOT a recommended portfolio — the equal-weight basket is an illustration, not your holdings.

The effective-bets figure is the participation ratio from the factor-baskets panel, which uses every day all 48 names share (about ten years, 2016–2026, including 2020 and 2022). On the embedded snapshot, 48 names collapse to about 4.8 effective bets. The diversification ratio, the top-3 share and the risk contributions use a separate covariance over the last 504 trading days (about two years) that all 48 names share, annualized; every name gets the same weight, and each name's risk contribution is its share of portfolio variance. The card repeats the absorption ratio, with its percentile in the rolling history of the same 252-day measure and the date it applies to ("now", "as of YYYY-MM-DD" when the data is stale, or the cursor date in Backtest), as a reminder of how much variance sits in a few modes; it is not itself a crisis reading (on the embedded snapshot it reads 79%, only the 12th percentile of its own history). Read it with Factor baskets: spreading money inside one basket barely diversifies.

Contrarian sentiment thermometer

Read it: One phrase — Panic / fear, Euphoria / greed, or Neutral — ignore — plus "proof it's not a clock": the spread of SPY's next-month returns from days like today.
Does NOT mean: NOT a turn signal, NOT "buy the panic", NOT a buy/sell call of any kind. Extremes persist and deepen for months (2000, 2021), and from here the share of up months is about SPY's usual share, with a losing lower quartile (the middle half of next-month outcomes runs from −0.8% to +2.5% around a +1.2% median on the embedded snapshot). It is context for rules you write down beforehand, never a trigger.
  • Panic / fear when VIX is at or above the 80th percentile of its own history, or turbulence is at least 80.
  • Euphoria / greed when CAPE (the embedded Shiller series, see valuation) is at or above its 85th percentile and VIX is at or below its 40th — the weaker leg, since CAPE carries ~zero short-horizon information.
  • Otherwise Neutral — ignore.

"Proof it's not a clock": the card takes every past day whose trailing 20-day SPY volatility fell in the same tercile as today's, and shows the 25th, 50th and 75th percentile of SPY's next-21-day return from those days, plus the share that were positive. (The bucket is the volatility tercile, not the sentiment phrase.) The "Share positive" row puts that bucket's share of up months next to SPY's share over all days (e.g. 68% vs 69% on the embedded snapshot). The point is that the bucket adds next to nothing over the base rate — it says little about direction — and its 25th percentile is still a loss, so both outcomes stay live. The card's caveat quotes that middle half (25th to 75th percentile) and the median from the same numbers, so it stays true when the bucket moves; it makes no turn or reversal claim. If prices keep falling after a panic reading, the reading was not wrong: a thermometer never claimed a turn.

Scenario stress-test — synthetic crises to stress-test a size you already chose

Read it: Two thousand synthetic next-month (21-trading-day) paths for SPY built from its own history: the 5th, 50th and 95th percentile of the month's return, and the worst dip within the month (median path, 5% tail, single worst path).
Does NOT mean: NOT a forecast of next month and NOT a probability you can bank on. It cannot invent a crisis worse than the moves it resamples — no novel black swan, and no 2008 (the embedded data starts in 2016). It is for stress-testing a position size you have already chosen.

Method — filtered historical simulation. Each of SPY's daily returns is divided by the EWMA volatility (λ = 0.94) prevailing at the time, giving a pool of volatility-standardized moves with the real fat tails. Each synthetic path draws 21 of those moves at random (independently, with a fixed seed) and rescales them to today's EWMA volatility. That gives a fuller range of bad months than the handful of real crises — the transparent cousin of a generative model, with no black box and no mode collapse.

Limits. Volatility is held at today's level for the whole path and the draws are independent, so the paths do not reproduce volatility clustering within the month; tails come only from the historical pool (2016 onward). It reads today's EWMA volatility only to scale this descriptive distribution: nothing on the card sets or changes a size. A stress distribution, never a prediction.

Crisis replay — your mix through past stress windows

Read it: Set the weights YOU choose across seven sleeves (Equities, Treasuries, Credit, Commodities, Gold, Dollar, Crypto), pick a real past stress window, and the card replays that allocation — daily-rebalanced — through the embedded history, showing an equity curve and the realized total return, max drawdown, and volatility versus 100%-stocks and a 60/40 baseline. It is a collapsed card on the Risk tab: click its title to open it.
Does NOT mean: NOT a forecast, NOT a recommendation, NOT an optimizer. It never suggests or optimizes weights — you set them. Past drawdown is not a prediction; the windows are a fixed, non-representative sample, and the embedded data starts in 2016 (there is no 2008 GFC in it).

This is the one new artifact that came out of asking whether a full Expected-Free-Energy planner could be added to the console. The honest answer was no — that planner needs a controllable agent, a learnable forward world-model, and stable structure, none of which markets give a passive observer (forward direction here is out-of-sample R²≈0). So this card is deliberately that planner with the planner, the world-model, and both free-energy terms removed: what's left is a pure historical replay, which is honest precisely because it asserts nothing about the future.

What it computes: for the chosen window, the portfolio return each day is the weighted sum of the sleeves' realized simple daily returns (renormalized across whichever sleeves have history — crypto only exists from late 2023, so it is dropped and flagged in earlier windows), cumulated into an equity curve. Max drawdown is the deepest peak-to-trough on that curve; volatility is the annualized standard deviation of daily returns. The 100%-stocks and 60/40 lines are the same computation on fixed reference weights.

Windows and sleeves: COVID crash (2020-02-19 → 2020-04-30), 2022 rate-shock bear (2022-01-03 → 2022-10-14), 2018 Q4 selloff (2018-09-20 → 2018-12-31), 2023 bank stress (2023-02-01 → 2023-05-01), 2024 August carry-unwind (2024-07-15 → 2024-09-10), and the full history (2016–2026). The sleeves map to SPY, TLT, LQD, DBC, GLD, UUP and BTC; presets are 60/40, Equal and All stocks. A sleeve with prices on fewer than 95% of the window's days is flagged. The weights you set here are also what the Drift & rebalance card reads.

What it teaches: the console's whole thesis, made personal. In the COVID window, a 60/40 mix cut the drawdown from about −34% (all stocks) to about −18% and the total loss from about −14% to about −1% — because Treasuries (the 40% TLT sleeve; the 60/40 baseline holds no gold) were still diversifying stocks. The lesson is to watch how much that protection shrinks in windows where everything couples (the systemic-coupling panel shows that coupling rising as it happens). It is a descriptive teaching tool for sizing and diversification intuition, not a trade.

Base-rate honesty check

This is the most integrity-preserving part of the console. Real financial crashes are rare (a handful per decade). With such a small sample, apparent "skill" in a backtest is mostly luck and overfitting. For the selected asset, over its whole history (the table does not move with the cursor), the check asks:

  • Unconditional rate: on all days, how often did a drawdown of at least 10% follow within the next 60 trading days?
  • Conditional rate: on days when fragility was ≥70, how often?
  • Time in alarm: what share of days had fragility ≥70?

Event counts sit next to each rate; no confidence interval is drawn, so read the counts. The verdict says the conditional rate is "somewhat above base rate … suggestive, not skill" only when it is more than 1.4× the base rate on at least two events; otherwise "no demonstrated out-of-sample timing skill". The console warns: "Crashes are rare (a handful of real events in a decade), so every rate above has a wide confidence interval; this is a base-rate honesty check, not a backtest of profits."

Why it borrows the water dashboard's lenses but not its foresight

Read it: Markets have no fixed topology, no accumulation, no exogenous drivers, and no hidden state. Every structural advantage the water system has for forecasting is inverted in markets.
Does NOT mean: Do not treat elevated readings as predictive signals. They show current stress, not future moves.

The Resilience Telemetry console applies several structural analogs from the Saint Petersburg SCADA water-monitoring system (which can warn hours ahead). However, four critical market differences prevent the same forward-looking skill:

1. Fixed topology vs. estimated and rewiring

Water: The pipe network and elevation structure are ground-truth, surveyed and stable. Pressure and flow respond to known fixed constraints.
Markets: The cross-asset correlation network is estimated from noisy daily returns and systematically rewires when a crisis hits. Historical "lead-lag" dissolves; everything converges to one mode (panic). The topology is a ghost until it breaks.

2. Conservation vs. price instantaneity

Water: Water piles up in tanks of known volume and travels pipes at known speed. Total level + rate-of-rise → calculable time-to-overflow.
Markets: Nothing accumulates. A crowded position unwinds in milliseconds through price. There is no quantity to integrate forward; only the expectation of future supply/demand is priced. A crowded trade can evaporate before any aggregating metric signals it.

3. Exogenous drivers with real lead time vs. endogenous and already priced

Water: Rain and surge are exogenous — coming whether or not anyone forecasts them, and don't change course because operators reacted to yesterday's forecast.
Markets: The "drivers" (Fed policy, CPI, flows) are already priced into expectations by the time they become observable. Information arbitrage across venues means any stable upstream-leads-downstream edge is traded to lag zero. Surprise is all that remains — by definition, unforecastable.

4. Hidden-state telemetry vs. price as the expectation

Water: SCADA reads a tank level only the operator can see. The gauge is independent of what happens next; observing it does not change the water level.
Markets: Price is the aggregate expectation. There is no hidden state to telemeter. Worse, observing a gauge — discussing a risk, hedging it, trading on it — immediately changes the expectation and the price. Markets exhibit reflexivity; water systems do not.

Consequence: thermometer, not forecast

The spectral radius (ρ̂), absorption ratio, turbulence, Hawkes branching ratio, and timed lead-lag are built as a systemic-stress thermometer. By their authors' own admission, these metrics rise during 2008, 2020, and 2022 — not before. They are coincident-to-lagging measures of stress, not predictive. The only weakly-leading construct is the standardized absorption-ratio shift, and even that is sample-dependent and has not held out-of-sample.

This is exactly why everything here stays descriptive, not predictive. The console tells you where the market sits today, in geometric and spectral terms, relative to its own history — not where it will go.

FAQ for investors & operators

Read it: High readings persist for years without crashing. Lead-lag is association, not causation. Closest analogs are geometric, not predictive. Descriptive, not predictive.
Does NOT mean: This is not a black-box trading system or a crash-prediction tool. It is a diagnostic for understanding current market stress.

Q: Is a high reading (fragility ≥70) a sell signal?

A: No. A high fragility reading is a descriptive statement: "This asset's stress indicators — persistence, amplitude, tail — have been rising faster than is usual for it." It does not predict when a drawdown will occur. Readings can stay high for months or years without significant correction; conversely, crashes sometimes begin from low readings. This is a stress thermometer, not a market-timing tool.

Q: I've seen elevated readings for months with nothing happening. Does that mean the console is broken?

A: No, that is expected and honest. With only a handful of real drawdown events in a decade, the base-rate chance of a crash on any given day is already low. An elevated thermometer reading may raise that chance somewhat, but from a small number. The Backtest / base-rate honesty panel quantifies this: if you find that fragility ≥70 raises the chance of a ≥10% drawdown within the next 60 trading days from (say) 6% to 8%, that is the true signal — modest and uncertain. The console will never claim otherwise.

Q: Does an arrow in the network mean A causes B?

A: No. An arrow i→j means i's past returns line up with j's future returns more than j's past lines up with i's future (lagged cross-correlation asymmetry). It is related to — but simpler than — the "Granger causality" tests of econometrics (it does not control for j's own past), and like them it is purely correlational. Causes can include: genuine information cascades (rare), different market-open times, measurement lag, or simple overfitting. Any stable edge gets arbitraged away; what you see is mostly the noise of the week.

Q: Is the "closest-analog" state a prediction?

A: No. The diffusion map shows that today's state is geometrically closest to (say) 2019 or 2021. That historical state is a coincident analog — it looked similar. But it does not predict that the same moves will follow. Markets have short memory, regime shifts can happen overnight, and new shocks are always possible.

Q: What does it mean if spectral radius is rising?

A: It means the cross-asset correlation matrix is concentrating its variance in fewer eigenmodes. Assets are moving more in sync. This is a real, measurable signal of systemic tightness — but it is a coincident signal, visible during 2020 and 2022 as the crisis unfolded, not cleanly ahead of time. Rising coupling makes the system more fragile to the next shock, but the shock's timing and magnitude are unforecastable.

Q: If the Hawkes branching ratio approaches 1, is a crash imminent?

A: No. A branching ratio near 1 (critical coupling) means large shocks are currently self-reinforcing — they beget more large shocks. This is a real description of the shock cascade active right now. But it does not tell you how long the cascade will last, how large the next shock will be, or when it will end. You are observing the stress, not predicting its duration.

Q: What if all the indicators are red and the diffusion map shows the stressed arm?

A: That is a coherent stress description: the market is exhibiting high fragility, tight coupling, strong shock clustering, and a geometric regime similar to past crises. It is useful context for risk managers — but by the console's own rule it is not a reason to trade: review hedge ratios, concentration and liquidity assumptions on the schedule and by the rules you set in advance, not because a gauge lit up. It does not tell you to sell everything immediately, nor does it guarantee a crash by Friday. It is an honest call to heightened vigilance, not to action.

Q: Can I backtest a strategy on these signals?

A: The Alpha Lab (Predictive tab) is exactly that: a net-of-cost, walk-forward backtest of a turbulence-triggered trade with a Deflated Sharpe and PBO check — and as built it finds no edge. Backtest / research mode adds a base-rate table for fragility (conditional vs unconditional drawdown rates with event counts; no confidence intervals are drawn, so read the counts). But be warned: with a dozen real drawdown events in 10 years, apparent backtest "skill" is mostly luck. And even an edge that seemed to survive testing would not change the console's rule: no reading here justifies a trade.

Q: Is the volatility channel different from GARCH?

A: Not meaningfully. Realized volatility (the amplitude sub-score) and the bimodality coefficient mostly restate volatility clustering, which GARCH models directly. If you run a GARCH on the same asset, you will see similar conditional-vol spikes. The surprise lens (AR(1)+EWMA-vol log-likelihood) is more independent, but still a simple model. Neither the volatility channel nor surprise are strong out-of-sample predictors of drawdowns.

Q: What does the covariance shrinkage α do in the turbulence panel?

A: It blends the sample covariance matrix with a scaled identity (every variance set to the average, all correlations zero) to reduce estimation noise when data is scarce or dimensions are high. Higher α = more shrinkage. The default (0.20) is moderate. If covariance becomes ill-conditioned (nearly singular), α auto-raises to at least 0.5. Adjust the slider to see how turbulence readings change with shrinkage, but remember: turbulence itself is a coincident metric, not forward-looking.

Q: Can I use this for real-time trading?

A: No. The console is for situational awareness, not trading — including "risk-management" trades. By its own rule no reading here may justify a trade: not tightening stops, adding hedges or cutting leverage because fragility or coupling looks high ("the market looks fragile" is never a reason). If you want risk controls, write them down in advance — the Risk tab's pre-commitments and your own volatility band — and let the rule act, not the gauge. Trying to trade the direction or timing of these signals will likely fail out-of-sample.

Glossary

Read it: See definitions of all key terms: spectral radius, absorption ratio, fragility thermometer, LCCA, diffusion map, Hawkes, turbulence, surprise, DFA, bimodality, and more.
Does NOT mean: These are technical terms; understanding them is important for interpreting the console correctly and avoiding false signals.
Absorption ratio
The fraction of total variance in a correlation matrix captured by the top k eigenmodes (Kritzman-Li metric; k = 10 for the console's 48 names). High ratio = little local shock-absorbing capacity; most variance is driven by a few shared modes. It is not a one-factor share (that is ρ̂). Where the console ranks today's value against its own history, it uses a rolling history of the same 252-day measure. Coincident, not leading.
AC1 (Lag-1 autocorrelation)
Correlation between today's return and yesterday's return. In efficient markets, ~0. Rising values suggest slower mean-reversion (critical-slowing-down analogue). Weak in returns; mostly restated by volatility clustering.
Backtest / research mode
Walk-forward computation for the panels that follow the cursor: at any cursor date, those statistics use only data up to that date. Several panels do not follow the cursor — the manifold (which has look-ahead), anticipation, the factor baskets, the Alpha Lab, forward-volatility lab and meta layer, valuation, the factor companion, overnight, crisis replay, the premia allocator, the eight Risk cards (one exception: the crash badge, the sentiment thermometer's Panic leg and the diversification card's absorption line reuse the turbulence, absorption and Hawkes readings as of the cursor date, mixed with latest-date inputs) and the live-only order book (see Live vs Backtest mode). The base-rate table, which does not move with the cursor either, compares actual forward drawdowns over the asset's whole history to the base rate. Strict honesty check against luck.
Base-rate honesty
A statistical comparison of how often a ≥10% drawdown followed within 60 trading days on days when fragility was ≥70 (conditional rate) vs. on any day (unconditional base rate). If conditional ≈ unconditional, there is no demonstrated timing skill.
Bimodality coefficient
Statistical measure of "two-humped" distribution shape. Rising bimodality suggests the asset is flickering between calm and stressed states. Often a false positive driven by GARCH vol clustering alone.
Branching ratio (Hawkes)
In a Hawkes self-exciting point process, the expected number of child shocks per parent shock. Ratio ~1 = critical (approaching instability); <<1 = shocks die away fast.
CAPE (cyclically adjusted P/E)
Shiller's price-to-earnings ratio using the 10-year average of inflation-adjusted earnings. Weakly informative about the next decade's real returns, useless at the console's 5–20-day horizon. Embedded through June 2026.
Critical-slowing-down (CSD)
In physics, the analogue of rising recovery time, persistence, and amplification near a phase transition. These metrics (AC1, DFA, rvol, bimodality) borrow the analogy from CSD, but in markets they rise during stress, not before, and are largely coincident or even lagging.
Cross-correlation asymmetry (LCCA)
Matrix A(i→j) = D(i→j) − D(j→i), where D(i→j) is the average absolute (Fisher-z) correlation of i's past returns with j's future returns over lags 1–4 — not a Granger test. Anti-symmetric: red when i leads j, blue when j leads i. Predictive association, not causation.
CSCV / PBO
Combinatorially symmetric cross-validation: cut the history into blocks, try every half-and-half split, and count how often the configuration that won in-sample lands below the median out-of-sample. That share is the probability of backtest overfitting (PBO). The Alpha Lab uses 10 blocks, without purging.
Deflated Sharpe ratio
The probability that a strategy's Sharpe ratio beats the best Sharpe you would expect from the same number of no-skill tries, adjusted for skew and fat tails (Bailey–López de Prado). It shrinks apparent edge; it never creates it.
Diffusion map
A nonlinear dimensionality-reduction technique (generalization of spectral decomposition / PCA) that embeds high-dimensional states (here, 10-day mean returns of the 48 non-crypto names) into a low-D space so that similar regimes cluster.
DFA (Detrended fluctuation analysis) / Hurst exponent α
Measures scaling of cumulative returns; α ≈0.5 = random walk (no persistence), α >0.5 = more persistence (slow mean-reversion), α <0.5 = mean-reverting. Needs long windows; lags regime shifts; mostly descriptive.
Directedness ratio (Σ|A|/ΣD)
A network statistic: sum of absolute asymmetries divided by total lagged cross-correlation magnitude. High ratio = clear leader-follower structure; low ratio = moves are simultaneous, with no clear leader. A common (simultaneous) shock adds co-movement without a leader, so it lowers the ratio or leaves it about unchanged — see the common-shock drill. Reads n/a when the network has no shared history (Backtest before 2023-09-21).
Eigenvalue (λmax)
The dominant (largest) eigenvalue of a correlation matrix. Normalized by N (number of assets), it gives the spectral radius ρ̂. Dominance = systemic coherence; in crises, ρ̂ rises as assets correlate to one mode.
Filtered historical simulation (FHS)
A stress-test method: standardize past returns by the volatility of their day, resample those standardized moves, and rescale them to today's volatility. Keeps real fat tails without a black-box model; cannot produce a crisis worse than the moves it resamples.
Fragility thermometer
A per-asset 0–100 score combining three channels (persistence, amplitude, tail) built from seven indicators, measuring how steeply those indicators have been rising over the last 60 days relative to the asset's own history. High ≠ crash; can persist for years. Descriptive, not predictive.
Granger causality / lead-lag
Statistical test of whether past values of X help forecast Y more than Y's own past alone. "Lead" means X's past is predictive for Y's future; "lag" means the reverse. The console's lead-lag is the simpler LCCA asymmetry, not a Granger test. In markets, either is association driven by mechanics, measurement lag, and overfitting, not true causation.
Hawkes process
A self-exciting point process where arrivals (shocks) increase the intensity of future arrivals (branching ratio n). Fitted to daily counts of ≥2.5σ (EWMA-standardized) shocks pooled across all 51 markets; branching ratio rises in stress.
Ill-conditioned covariance
When a covariance matrix is nearly singular (near-zero or negative eigenvalues), inverse estimates blow up. Shrinkage α or dimension reduction is applied. The console auto-raises α to at least 0.5 if the matrix becomes ill-conditioned.
Mahalanobis distance / turbulence
One-day multivariate generalization of z-score. Measures how unusual today's cross-asset move is relative to the recent covariance. Risen in 2020/2022 (coincident), not before. An active-inference analogue (variational free energy) of per-asset surprise.
Manifold / state geometry
The high-dimensional space spanned by historical market states (here, 48-asset 10-day mean returns). A diffusion map embeds it into 2-D; similar regimes cluster. Coincident description of the market's regime geometry, not predictive of next state.
Participation ratio
The effective number of independent factors in a correlation matrix: (Σλ)² / Σλ² over its eigenvalues, i.e. trace² / ‖C‖². 48 names with a participation ratio of about 4.8 behave like fewer than five independent bets.
Permutation-gated null
Statistical test: circularly shift every series by its own random offset (160 draws) to break any lead-lag timing, recompute the asymmetries, and count a link i→j as significant only if its asymmetry exceeds the 95th percentile of its null values. Each pair is tested on its own at about the 5% level with no multiple-testing correction, so across 1,275 pairs a few dozen passes are expected by chance; the network draws the 12 strongest asymmetries and marks the passing ones solid.
Realized volatility (rvol)
The standard deviation of daily returns over a window, annualized (√(252 × variance)). A measure of magnitude of fluctuations. Rising rvol mostly restates GARCH vol clustering; a coincident metric, not predictive.
Recovery rate λ = −ln φ
Derived from AR(1) coefficient φ. λ measures the rate of exponential reversion to mean; rising λ = faster recovery. Sibling of AC1; largely redundant information in markets.
Shrinkage (α)
Regularization parameter for covariance estimation: C_shrink = (1−α)×C_sample + α×(tr C / N)×I. Higher α = more smoothing, better condition number, but less responsiveness to true covariance changes. Default 0.20; auto-raised to at least 0.5 if ill-conditioned.
Snapshot (embedded real history)
Daily closes for all 51 markets from 2016 (FX from 2015; ~3.1 years of crypto) to 20–21 October 2026, plus embedded FRED macro series, open/close data for five funds and Shiller CAPE through June 2026. Baked into the HTML; always available even if live feeds fail.
Spectral radius ρ̂
The dominant eigenvalue of the rolling cross-asset correlation matrix, normalized by N. Measures systemic coupling: ρ̂→1 = tight coupling (all move together); ρ̂ low = loose coupling (independent shocks). Coincident stress metric, not predictive.
Surprise lens (active-inference analogue)
One-step negative log-likelihood of a simple AR(1)+EWMA-volatility model. Rising = today's moves are more surprising to a simple expectation model. An active-inference variational-free-energy analogue, not the full EFE engine. Per-asset, not systemic.
Walk-forward validation
Strict backtesting methodology: at any in-sample date, all parameters are estimated using only data up to that date. Forward metrics are computed on future data. Prevents look-ahead bias. The console's Backtest mode does this recomputation only for the panels that follow the cursor — not for the manifold, anticipation, the factor baskets, the labs and meta layer, valuation, the factor companion, overnight, crisis replay, the premia allocator, the Risk cards (apart from the cursor-date turbulence, absorption and Hawkes readings that three of them reuse, mixed with latest-date inputs) or the order book (full list under Live vs Backtest mode).

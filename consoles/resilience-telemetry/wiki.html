What this is — and what it is NOT

Read it: Start here. The console is explicitly NOT predictive. It is a stress thermometer, not a crash detector.
Does NOT mean: Do NOT use this to time trades or forecast crashes. Do NOT assume high readings mean a collapse is imminent. Do NOT treat lead-lag associations as causal relationships.

The Honesty Contract. This console describes financial stress in real time. It is not a crash predictor, not a timing tool, and makes no forecast. The framing borrows from physics (critical slowing down, phase transitions) as an analogy only — these signals are mostly absent before historical meltdowns.

Why Markets Aren't Like the Water System. The console's core panels — spectral radius, absorption ratio, turbulence, Hawkes clustering, lead-lag — were built as a thermometer of stress, not a thermometer of future crisis. By their own authors' admission, they rise during 2008/2020/2022, not before. Markets invert every advantage the water system has:

  • Topology is estimated and rewires in a crisis. The cross-asset network here is inferred from noisy returns; the instant volatility spikes, correlations collapse to one mode (everything moves together), and the graph rewires unpredictably.
  • Nothing is conserved. A crowded position unwinds in milliseconds through price. There is no quantity to integrate forward — no water level rising to a calculable overflow.
  • Drivers are endogenous and already priced. The would-be exogenous shocks (Fed policy, CPI, flows) are already embedded in price expectations. Only the unforecastable surprise remains.
  • Information has ~zero transport delay. Any stable upstream-leads-downstream pattern gets arbitraged away immediately. Lead-lag is an association observed in the data, not a causal edge you can exploit.
  • Price is the expectation itself. There is no hidden state. Watching the gauge perturbs it (reflexivity). The market is not a system with secrets to uncover.

What the Panels Actually Tell You. When the Fragility Thermometer rises (60–100), or spectral radius climbs, or Mahalanobis turbulence spikes, you are seeing that the market is currently moving in ways that are statistically large, persistent, or tightly coupled — today. A high reading can persist for months or years with nothing catastrophic happening. It can also drop from low levels with no warning. This is coincident, not leading.

The Only Weakly-Honest Leading Signal. The standardized absorption-ratio shift has shown the barest hint of lead in some sample windows — but it is sample-dependent and has not held out-of-sample. Do not trade on it.

The header regime line

Under the title you'll see, e.g., Regime: Elevated — watch · coupling moderate · turbulence calm · data as of 2026-06-16 (in Backtest the last part reads cursor YYYY-MM-DD). That's the one-glance summary: the overall regime word, whether assets are moving together (coupling — high at or above the 65th percentile of its own history, moderate from the 40th, otherwise low), whether today's combined move is unusual (turbulence — high at ≥85, elevated at ≥60, otherwise calm), and the date the cross-asset data runs to.

The regime word is systemic, not one stock

The headline regime — Calm / Slightly elevated / Elevated — watch / Stressed — is driven by the system: how tightly assets are coupled, how large the combined move is, and how many assets are individually elevated. Stressed means turbulence ≥85, coupling at or above its 85th percentile, or at least 45% of markets with fragility ≥60; Elevated — watch uses 60 / 65th / 25%; Slightly elevated uses 45 / 45th / 12%; otherwise Calm. A single very-stretched name does not turn the whole headline red (that name is still called out in the first sentence). The colored pill in the top-right corner, by contrast, flags the single least-resilient asset.

What each sentence covers

  • Overall + most stretched name — the regime, and which single market is most outside its own norm.
  • Coupling — are markets moving independently or together, and the plain consequence: when they move together, diversification protects you less.
  • Absorption-ratio shift (only when it is at least 1σ above its baseline) — the way markets share risk is reorganising; the one construct with a weak tendency to lead, sample-dependent and coincident in practice.
  • Turbulence — is today's combined move ordinary or unusually large.
  • Leaders — which market's moves have tended to come first lately, and the market it leads most strongly (with the reminder: a pattern, not a cause, and it can flip). If directedness is low it says no single market is clearly leading.
  • Closest historical analog — which past date today's market "shape" most resembles (a resemblance, not a prediction).
  • Shock clustering — whether big moves are isolated or clustering.
  • Anticipation — which market's stress tends to lead the others (the Diebold–Yılmaz transmitter), whether it held a small out-of-sample lead, and whether that leadership is stable — framed as where to look, still not a trade signal.

Every readout ends with the same bottom line: it describes conditions, it does not forecast a crash, and it is not advice.

Directed spillover network (LCCA) — who leads whom

Read it: Diagnostic tab, first card. Left = net transmitters (red), right = net receivers (blue). Only solid arrows passed the permutation gate; the small label on each is the lag (days) of its peak. Most surviving leads peak at 1 day — effectively contemporaneous. Lead-lag here is association, not causation.
Does NOT mean: Not causal graphs. Not a predictor of spillover events. Not a ranking of systemic importance—transmitters may be volatility conduits, not fundamental drivers. The network rewires in crises; historical in-sample links often don't hold out-of-sample. Lead-lag is dominated by common risk factors and information lags, not structural causality.

What Is LCCA?

Lagged Cross-Correlation Asymmetry: measures whether one asset's past returns line up with another's future returns more than the reverse. An arrow i→j means i's lagged returns correlate more strongly with j's forward returns than the reverse, over the last 252 trading days that all 51 markets share.

Calculation

For each pair i, j and lags k = 1 to L (L = 4 days):

  • Compute Fisher z-transformed cross-correlation: xcorr(i, j, k) at lag k.
  • Average absolute z-values: D(i→j) = Σ|fz(xcorr(i, j, k))| / L.
  • Asymmetry: A(i→j) = D(i→j) − D(j→i). Red i→j means i leads j; blue j leads i; near-white = pure co-movement.
  • Net transmitter/receiver: NET(i) = Σⱼ A(i→j). Positive = transmitter (red), negative = receiver (blue).

Permutation Gate

Each of 160 null draws circularly shifts every series by its own random offset — destroying the lead-lag timing while keeping each series' own behaviour — and recomputes the asymmetries. A link passes only if its asymmetry exceeds the 95th percentile of that pair's 160 null values. The draws use a fixed seed, so the same data always gives the same gate. Solid arrows = significant under the gate; faint dashed = strongest tendencies below significance.

Directedness Ratio

Σ|A| / ΣD—how much of the total cross-correlation is directional (asymmetric) vs co-movement. Higher = clearer leader-follower structure; lower = assets moving as one, simultaneously. The console computes it only for the current window (no history of it is plotted), so this guide makes no claim about how it behaves through crises; the common-shock drill in the Scenario sandbox shows that simultaneous moves push it down.

Critical Honesty Note

Lead-lag is association, not causation. Most surviving leads peak at 1 day (≈contemporaneous), reflecting information diffusion and microstructure, not causal transmission. With the Scenario sandbox (in either mode) you can inject a synthetic lead to watch the network respond—but real lead-lag tendencies are fleeting and don't generalize out-of-sample. The network is a snapshot of dependence structure, not a transmission mechanism.

Display

  • Network diagram: nodes ordered from the strongest transmitter (left) to the strongest receiver (right), sized by |NET|, filled red/blue by sign and ringed in their factor-basket colour. Edges = the 12 largest positive asymmetries; solid edges carry the lag (1–8 days) at which that pair's lagged correlation peaks. A "DRILL — SYNTHETIC" badge appears while a drill is active. On phones the diagram scrolls sideways inside its card.
  • Heat matrix: rows = source i, cols = target j. Red (i→j) = i leads; blue = j leads i. Intensity = strength of the z-transformed correlation asymmetry.

Asymmetry matrix & net transmitter / receiver

Read it: The N×N heatmap (rows = source, columns = target) shows which asset leads which. The bar chart to the right ranks all assets by net directedness: red extends right (transmits), blue extends left (receives).
Does NOT mean: The values are correlation-based associations, not causal drivers. A red SPY→LQD link does not mean stock strength causes bond weakness; it means their lagged returns moved together in that direction during the window — and only links drawn solid on the network diagram passed the circular-shift gate; matrix cells are not gated.

The left panel shows the directed asymmetry matrix A(i→j) = D(i→j) − D(j→i), where D(i→j) is the average absolute (Fisher-z) lagged cross-correlation of i leading j over lags 1–4.

Matrix interpretation:

  • Red cells (positive A) indicate i→j: asset i leads asset j.
  • Blue cells (negative A) indicate j→i: asset j leads asset i.
  • Near-white cells indicate pure co-movement with no directional winner (both lead/lag symmetrically).

The matrix is anti-symmetric: if A(SPY→LQD) is red, then A(LQD→SPY) is equally blue. Row and column labels use the ticker (FX pairs by their quote currency, e.g. JPY).

Right panel — net directional spillover: A table ranks assets by their NET score (sum of all outgoing leads minus all incoming lags), with a centred bar:

  • Right/red bars: Pure sources and hubs that lead more than they lag.
  • Left/blue bars: Pure sinks and relays that lag more than they lead.
  • TO[i] = total outgoing lead-lag contribution (i leads others).
  • FROM[i] = total incoming lead-lag contribution (others lead i).

Which markets transmit and which receive changes with the window, so there is no stable "typical" pattern to memorise. On the embedded snapshot (markets as of 2026-06-16), NVDA, EM sovereign debt (EMB) and SPY are the largest net transmitters, and the strongest receivers are the FX pairs (USD vs AUD, EUR and CHF).

Cascade simulation — shock propagation

Read it: Pick a market to shock and a minimum link strength; the panel propagates the shock downstream along the directed spillover network and reports how many markets it reaches, in how many waves, and which fall in each wave. It also reports the percolation threshold — the strongest link-strength cut at which a shock from the chosen market still reaches at least half of all markets.
Does NOT mean: NOT a forecast and NOT a claim that shocking X will hit Y. The network is estimated from noisy returns, it rewires in a crisis, and lead-lag is association arbitraged toward lag 0. This is a descriptive what-if drill on the current inferred structure.

This is your St. Pete water‑cascade mechanic — directed downstream propagation — brought to the market network as a directed‑reachability (percolation) cascade, the same family of model used for epidemics, power‑grid blackouts, and bank runs. A shock at the source flows to any market reachable via directed lead‑lag links of at least the chosen strength; each wave is one hop further out. Raising the trigger keeps only the strongest links (the cascade localises); lowering it lets weaker links carry the shock system‑wide. The behaviour is genuinely source‑dependent: a shock at a hub like the S&P spreads in a few waves across dozens of markets, while a shock at a peripheral market like agriculture dies almost immediately. The percolation line is the tipping point at which one shock spans ≥50% of the system — the cascade‑analysis read on systemic fragility. It is descriptive: it shows how the currently inferred structure would carry a shock, not whether one is coming.

Settings. Link strength is measured relative to the strongest asymmetry in the network: at the default 40% (slider 15–65%) only links with A(i→j) at least 40% of the largest A are used. Every positive asymmetry above the cut counts — the cascade does not apply the network's permutation gate. The source selector is grouped by factor basket and starts on the anticipation transmitter. The percolation line scans cuts from 90% down to 5% in 5-point steps and reports the highest one at which the chosen source still reaches ≥50% of markets (or "never spans ≥50%").

Network structure — hubs, choke points & amplification

Read it: Three structural reads of the directed spillover network: where outflow concentrates (preferential-attachment hubs), which market is the bottleneck whose removal most fragments how a shock spreads (choke point), and how far a typical shock travels (amplifier vs suppressor).
Does NOT mean: NOT a forecast. The network is estimated and rewires; these are descriptive structure of the current inference. The choke point is not a "weak link that will break" — it is where the current inferred topology happens to bottleneck.

This panel is the honest residue of two more cross-domain analogies — metabolic pathways and evolutionary/social graph theory. Preferential attachment (the rich-get-richer power law seen in wealth and in how well-connected mutant genes take over a structured population) shows up as spillover concentration: a Gini and top-3 share of out-strength, and the hub list. Choke points are the metabolic-pathway idea of bottleneck reactions: the market whose removal most fragments shock propagation — which is genuinely distinct from the loudest transmitter (on the embedded snapshot, the top transmitter is NVDA while the top choke points are MSFT and EM sovereign debt). Amplification is evolutionary graph theory's amplifier-vs-suppressor distinction: how far a single shock reaches on average. What deliberately did not make it: flux-balance analysis (the predictive core of metabolic modelling) needs a conserved, stoichiometric quantity — markets have none (their #2 missing ingredient); and fixation-probability / evolutionary-dynamics models need an observable population of agents or strategies, which price data does not contain. Importing either would manufacture prediction the substrate can't support — so only the descriptive network structure transfers.

How it is computed. A market's out-strength is the sum of its positive asymmetries; the panel shows the top-3 share of all outflow, the Gini of out-strength and the five biggest hubs. Choke points: each of the 12 strongest hubs is removed in turn and the console measures how much total shock reach falls; the top four are listed. Amplification is the average share of markets a single shock reaches (≥40% = amplifier, ≥20% = mild amplifier, otherwise suppressor). Both use a fixed 40% link-strength cut, independent of the cascade slider.

Systemic coupling — spectral radius ρ̂ & absorption ratio

Read it: The 'Systemic coupling' panel shows two sparklines and current values: ρ̂ (spectral radius) and AR (absorption ratio). Both are plotted over time since 2016; the caveats explain they rise DURING crises (2020 and 2022 in the sparklines; 2008 in the wider literature), not before.
Does NOT mean: These are NOT early-warning signals of a crash. They are 'thermometer' readings of how unified the market currently is, analogous to critical-slowing-down in physics, but the analogy breaks in markets because the topology is estimated post-hoc, not fixed as in water systems.

These two metrics measure how tightly the whole cross-asset system is moving as a single unified entity. Both are coincident-to-lagging indicators: they spike during stress, not before.

Spectral radius ρ̂: The dominant eigenvalue (λ_max) of the rolling cross-asset correlation matrix, divided by the number of assets. Think of it as a proxy for how many independent modes of variation remain in the system. High ρ̂ (closer to 1) means the assets are "mode-collapsing" — correlations rising, individual shock absorption falling.

  • Formula: ρ̂ = λ_max / N (where N is the asset count).
  • This is an analogy to the VAR spectral radius ρ(Φ) from your seismic/hydraulic work, not a fitted dynamical operator.
  • The basis is the 48 non-crypto names (US and international equities, sectors, rates, credit, commodities, the dollar, FX and VIX); crypto is excluded so the history reaches back to 2016.
  • Current values use the last 252 trading days all 48 share; the sparklines are rolling 60-day windows stepped every 7 days across the whole history.

Absorption ratio (AR): The fraction of total variance explained by the top k eigenmodes, with k = N/5 = 10 for the 48 names. High AR = little local shock absorption; most of the move is concentrated in the leading modes. Kritzman-Li (2011) framed this as a fragility measure, but it too rises during crises, not before. The standardized AR-shift δ compares the mean of the three latest rolling AR values with the mean and spread of all earlier ones (a weak regime flag only). The panel also lists the eight largest loadings on the dominant mode — what drives the common factor.

Why they are not leading indicators: Both metrics are fixed-topology readouts of realized correlation. When liquidity evaporates or systematic factors dominate (as in March 2020 or September 2022), all correlations spike simultaneously — the topology doesn't exist until after the move. The console's transparency: "ρ̂ is an analogy to the water system's fixed topology — except here the topology is estimated and rewires in a crisis."

What they tell you: High ρ̂/AR indicates the current market is moving in lock-step (regime of tight coupling), not that a break is imminent. A persistent high reading can last months or years with nothing happening; declines also start from low readings (base-rate honesty).

Factor baskets — what actually moves together

Read it: Hierarchical (average-linkage) clustering of the return-correlation matrix groups the 48 non-crypto names into a handful of empirical factors that actually drive them — the "actual baskets," not the nominal nine asset-class labels. It reports the participation ratio (effective number of independent factors), and the directed-network nodes are ringed in each basket's color.
Does NOT mean: Assets in one basket are NOT independent bets. Spreading money across names inside the same basket barely diversifies. The clustering is descriptive of co-movement, not a claim about causation or the future.

This panel answers "how much of my breadth is real?" Directly: the 48 non-crypto names collapse to about 4.7 effective independent factors (participation ratio = trace²/‖C‖²). The biggest basket — roughly two-thirds of the assets (33 of 48) — is a single global equity beta cluster: US large-cap, sectors, developed and emerging international, high-yield/EM credit and even broad commodities and crude oil (DBC, USO) all move as one risk-on factor. The clustering is genuinely informative where the labels mislead: it splits "credit" by behavior — high-yield and EM debt land in the equity-beta basket, while investment-grade and government bonds plus gold form a separate duration / safe-haven basket. The dollar/FX names form a third basket; volatility (VIX), cash (SHV) and agriculture (CORN) each sit alone. This is the empirical backbone of the "more assets, not more information" finding, shown as structure rather than asserted.

How it is computed. Correlations use every day all 48 names share; distance = 1 − ρ; clusters are merged by average linkage until exactly six remain (the number of baskets is fixed at six, not discovered), and the card shows each basket's size and average internal correlation. Crypto is not clustered; it is added as a seventh, display-only "Crypto" group. The baskets are computed once when the page loads.

The baskets drive the whole interface. The asset selector is grouped into these baskets (not the nominal classes), the fragility thermometer sorts and headers by basket with a basket-color dot on each row, and the directed-network nodes are ringed in their basket color — so wherever you look, assets are organized by what actually moves together. (The underlying class field is retained only for mechanics like base-rate eligibility, the crypto exclusion and FX labelling.)

Game-theoretic lens — beauty contest & coordination risk

Read it: Two things. First, the strategic reason short-term direction can't be forecast (Keynes' beauty contest / higher-order beliefs). Second, a coordination/run-vulnerability gauge built from existing readings — strategic complementarity (coupling), crowding (share of markets already stretched), and whether the system's leader (focal point) is stable.
Does NOT mean: NOT a prediction that a run or crash will occur. The run-vulnerability gauge is a global-games framing that reads conditions off the cross-asset state — it never claims a coordinated move is imminent.

This makes the game theory that was implicit in the console explicit. Why direction is a guessing game: a price is the market's average guess about everyone else's average guess (Keynes' beauty contest); acting on a public signal moves the very price you are predicting (reflexivity); and any stable edge is competed toward zero. That higher-order-belief loop is the game-theoretic reason short-term direction is a martingale — the console's whole thesis, restated in game theory. Coordination / run-vulnerability: a crisis is a coordination failure (a run, a stampede, a currency attack). Global-games theory (Morris–Shin) says such failures get likelier when actions strongly reinforce each other (high complementarity ≈ high coupling), many players already sit near their tipping point (crowding), and the focal point is shifting (an unstable system leader). The gauge blends those three into a descriptive vulnerability read — context for the regime, not a timing signal.

The blend: 0–100 = 50% × coupling percentile + 30% × share of markets with fragility ≥60 + 20% if the anticipation transmitter is unstable across bootstrap resamples. Below 40 reads low, 40–65 moderate, 66 and above elevated.

Resting bid vs ask size — live book microstructure

Read it: Diagnostic tab, live mode only. For BTC, ETH and SOL: a centred bar showing whether more resting size sits on the bid or on the ask of the Binance.US order book (top 20 levels), refreshed every 5 seconds, plus BTC's top-of-book spread in basis points.
Does NOT mean: NOT a buy or sell call and NOT a short-term forecast. Any move a skewed book anticipates is smaller than the spread and gone in seconds — not directional alpha, and not tradable through a browser. A skewed book is liquidity resting there right now.

Imbalance = (bid size − ask size) / (bid size + ask size) over the top 20 price levels, shown on a ±100 scale ("balanced" within ±5, otherwise bid- or ask-skewed). Read it: Read the turbulence value and its χ² percentile, then the list of today's biggest contributors. Treat the percentile as ordinal (fat tails make it optimistic). The shrinkage slider changes how much the covariance is pulled toward a scaled identity (equal variances, zero correlations).
Does NOT mean: Not a forecast of volatility or crashes. Not a threshold-crossing predictor—high turbulence can persist for days with no event, or spike on a single outlier. Core Measure

The Mahalanobis distance of today's one-day cross-asset return vector (the 48 non-crypto names) from the mean of the last 252 shared trading days, scaled by that window's covariance. A multivariate extension of the per-asset surprise lens, capturing how far the system sits from its typical state in metric-adjusted space.

Calculation

  • Estimate mean μ and covariance Σ from the last 252 trading days all 48 names share (at least 3 × 48 = 144 are needed).
  • Shrink: Σα = (1−α)Σ + α·(tr Σ / N)·I — a fixed linear shrinkage toward equal variances and zero correlation (not Ledoit-Wolf's estimated intensity). α is the slider (0–0.8, default 0.20); if the matrix still cannot be inverted, the console raises α to at least 0.5 and says so.
  • Compute d = (x − μ)ᵀ Σα⁻¹ (x − μ) for today's one-day return vector x (and for every day in the window, for the sparkline).
  • Map today's d to a χ² CDF percentile using N = 48 degrees of freedom.
  • The sparkline is the 20-day rolling average of d across the window.

Interpretation

Higher turbulence = larger deviation from the system's covariance-weighted center. Rising turbulence during 2020 and 2022 crises is well-documented; it is a coincident-to-slightly-lagging read on regime stress, not a leading signal.

The drivers list shows each asset's contribution to today's Mahalanobis distance: xᵢ × (Σ⁻¹ x)ᵢ, ranked by size (top eight) — the assets pushing the system away from its center.

The console's headline score: a composite 0–100 reading per asset, built from seven indicators grouped into three channels. They are not independent; they overlap significantly, especially the persistence measures.

How the score is built

  1. Seven indicators on a rolling 252-day window of daily log returns: lag-1 autocorrelation and the recovery rate λ = −ln φ (both on EWMA-volatility-standardized residuals), DFA / Hurst α (recomputed every 3 days), annualized realized volatility, the bimodality coefficient, skewness and excess kurtosis (see Indicator detail).
  2. Direction-adjust so that higher = more fragile (λ and skewness are sign-flipped).
  3. Trend, not level: take each indicator's 60-day OLS slope and turn it into an expanding z-score against that asset's own history of slopes.
  4. Three channels: persistence = mean of the AC1, λ and DFA z-scores; amplitude = mean of realized vol and bimodality; tail = mean of skewness and kurtosis.

Display: a basket-colour dot, the score bar (green below 40, light green 40–59, amber 60–84, red 85+), the value, and the three mini-bars. Honest caveats per channel:

  • Persistence (AC1, λ, DFA): AC1 is the weakest channel in markets; λ carries the same information as AC1; DFA needs long windows and lags the regime shift.
  • Amplitude (realized vol, bimodality): mostly restates known volatility clustering (GARCH); often coincident, not leading; apparent flickering has a high false-positive rate.
  • Tail (skewness, kurtosis): one extreme day flips or dominates the estimators; very noisy.

Why the critical-slowing-down analogy breaks: Physics systems (lasers, neurons) show genuine early warning via reduced variance and coherence before a bifurcation. Markets invert this: price is the expectation; there's no hidden state to telemeter. Correlations are estimated and rewire instantly on news, so no fixed topology exists until after the move. The console is unsparing: "The critical-slowing-down framing is an analogy from physics (these signals were largely absent before historical meltdowns); the volatility channel mostly restates GARCH."

Surprise lens (active-inference analogue)

Read it: One bar per market: how surprising its last ~20 days of moves were to a simple causal AR(1)+EWMA-volatility model (0–100 vs its own history). High = recent moves keep beating that simple model's expectations — a coincident read, not a warning.
Does NOT mean: Not a leading indicator. Not volatility or variance—it is a residual innovation metric conditioned on a 60-day local model. Not a forecast of returns or crashes. Not independent of fragility—both rise during stress, but neither causes the other.

Core Concept

One-step negative log-likelihood (NLL) from a simple AR(1) + EWMA model. Analogue of variational free energy from active inference: higher surprise = larger deviation from the asset's local predictive model. Reported as a 0–100 reading: today's 20-day-smoothed NLL standardized against the mean and spread of all earlier smoothed values for that asset, mapped through the normal CDF.

Calculation

For each day t ≥ 1:

  • Fit the AR(1) coefficient φ on a rolling 60-day window ending at t−1.
  • Predict next day: μ = φ × r[t−1].
  • Compute NLL: 0.5 × ((r[t] − μ) / σ)² + log(σ), where σ = √v and v is a running EWMA variance (started from the first 60 returns).
  • Update variance via EWMA: v ← 0.94 × v + 0.06 × (r[t] − μ)².
  • Smooth with 20-day rolling average.
  • Convert to a 0–100 reading using the mean and std of the smoothed series up to that day (walk-forward).

Interpretation

High surprise (70–100) = asset behavior deviates far from its own simple local predictive model. Not a forecast signal, but a coincident read on how much the asset is surprising itself. During stress (2020, 2022), surprise tends to rise, along with vol and fragility.

Why "Active Inference"?

In active inference, the system minimizes surprise (prediction error) to maintain a generative model. Here, we invert it: surprise measures how much the asset's local model is failing. Rising surprise = environment is drifting away from the model, not that a crash is imminent.

Shock clustering — Hawkes self-excitation

Read it: Read the system branching ratio n (0 = shocks isolated, → 1 = each shock triggers about one more) and the shock half-life, then the per-asset list. High n means aftershocks are under way now — not that a new shock is coming.
Does NOT mean: Not a predictor of the next shock. Not a measure of systemic importance or contagion. High branching ratio = feedback loops are active, not that a crash is coming. n is very noisy with few events; a single bad day changes the fit significantly. Not independent of turbulence or fragility—all three rise together during crises, but none causes the others.

Core Concept

Do large moves beget more large moves? Fits a self-exciting point process to large return shocks (≥2.5σ magnitude, measured against each asset's EWMA volatility). The branching ratio n is how self-reinforcing the clustering is: n ≈ 0.3 means shocks decay; n ≈ 0.9 means critical (on the brink of runaway); n = 1 is unstable.

Calculation

  • For each of the 51 assets, standardize daily returns by an EWMA volatility (λ = 0.94) and mark days with |z| above 2.5 as shocks.
  • Pool all assets' shocks into one daily count over the last 751 calendar days (ending today, or at the cursor); weekends count as zero-shock days.
  • Fit Hawkes process: λ(t) = μ + α Σ exp(−β(t − tᵢ)), where μ = background rate, α = jump size, β = decay rate — in discrete daily time, by maximizing a Poisson log-likelihood.
  • Grid search over (μ, α, β) — 6 × 6 × 5 values — then four rounds of local refinement.
  • Compute branching ratio: n = α exp(−β) / (1 − exp(−β)). This is the expected number of children per event in a Galton-Watson branching process.
  • Also report half-life: how fast aftershocks decay (log(2) / β, in calendar days).
  • Repeat the fit per asset for every asset with at least 8 shocks in the window.

Interpretation

CONTEMPORANEOUS: high n = shocks CURRENTLY clustering (aftershocks underway), NOT a coming exogenous mainshock. Rising branching ratio during stress (2022) means the cascade is self-sustaining. However, a high n does not forecast the next shock's magnitude or timing—only that if a shock occurs, more are likely to follow quickly. Implements your LSAxJEPA Hawkes λᵢ(t); the temporal twin of ρ(Φ) → 1 (cf. the induced-seismicity pilot).

Caveats

  • Least-stable panel: single exponential kernel, daily bins, often few jump events per asset (per-asset fits need ≥8). Confidence in n is low.
  • Threshold-dependent: moving 2.5σ to 3.0σ or 2.0σ (the console fixes it at 2.5) changes the count and fit.
  • Temporal resolution: daily bins may miss intra-day clustering and aftershock sequences that resolve in hours.

Display

The system branching ratio n with a 0–100 bar (clipped at 1.0) and a sparkline of the fitted intensity, the shock half-life, the total shock count, then bars for the eight assets with the highest per-asset n. A message appears if there are too few shocks (five or fewer in total) to fit a reliable process.

Anticipation — where stress starts, & the out-of-sample test (the predictive layer)

Read it: This panel is the companion paper's actual engine made live. It names the upstream transmitter (the market whose stress tends to lead the others — where to direct attention, not a trade), contrasts it with the loudest (most volatile) market, and runs a genuine out-of-sample test of whether the transmitter actually leads.
Does NOT mean: A buy/sell signal or a price/return forecast. Any out-of-sample lead it finds is small in absolute terms — it sharpens where stress originates, not whether to trade.

Every other panel is descriptive — it tells you the state now. This one is the closest thing to a predictive reading the evidence honestly supports, and it comes straight from the companion research paper ("Directedness, not connectedness, decides when to anticipate a contagion").

What it computes

  • Net transmitter (Diebold–Yılmaz): a one-lag vector autoregression (lightly ridge-regularized) on the standardized absolute daily returns of the 48 long-history macro names, decomposed with a generalized forecast-error-variance decomposition over 8 days into directed volatility spillovers. The market that exports more stress than it imports (highest NET) is the transmitter — the "anticipation target": the place to watch, for attention, not for trades.
  • Loudest market: the basis market with the highest mean absolute daily return over the full sample — the most volatile name, the instinctive "react here" target, which is usually downstream. For the out-of-sample test it is re-picked on the training split.
  • Total directed spillover (Diebold–Yılmaz connectedness, %) and directedness (0–1): how one-way the pairwise spillovers are. The paper's core finding: anticipation has structural leverage only when the network is directionally asymmetric — when the transmitter and the loudest market differ. The verdict line: if the transmitter is the loudest, reacting is as good as anticipating; if total spillover is below 35% or directedness below 0.12, there is no clear upstream source; otherwise the transmitter exports stress the loudest market receives — worth watching, for attention, not for trades.
  • Transmitter stability: 100 block-bootstrap refits report how often the same market comes out on top and a 95% interval for its rank, plus a 95% bootstrap interval for its NET. If it tops fewer than half the resamples or its rank interval reaches past 3, the panel (and the briefing) call the leadership unstable.

The out-of-sample test (why this is honest, not hype)

The engine splits the shared history into a training part (first 60%) and a held-out test part (last 40%). It picks the transmitter and the loudest market on the training part only, then checks on the unseen test part how well each one's stress today correlates with the whole basket's average stress five days later. It reports both correlations, the transmitter's advantage, and the share of 200 block-bootstrap resamples of the test period in which that advantage is positive.

The verdict has three branches: positive in at least 90% of resamples → "a modest, reasonably robust out-of-sample lead" (still small in absolute terms); positive but less robust → "positive but not robust", echoing the companion paper's own real-data result (positive but not statistically significant); otherwise → no out-of-sample lead on this split. Either way it sharpens where stress tends to originate — exactly why the panel refuses to call it a trade signal. Lead is association, not causation, and on non-stationary data the transmitter migrates over time — read the stability rows.

The panel also carries a methods caveat: variance spillover can distort linear Granger-type inference (Mantalos & Shukur 2010); this is a forecast-error-variance decomposition, not impulse-response causality, and a nonlinear-Granger check (Wismüller et al. 2021) is the robustness alternative. The engine runs on the full history — it does not follow the Backtest cursor.

The Run rigorous validation button sweeps 15 configurations (3 stances × τ at the 50th, 60th, 70th, 80th and 90th percentiles, at your current cost, holding period and size) and reports two overfitting diagnostics: a Deflated Sharpe Ratio (Bailey–López de Prado — the probability that your configuration's Sharpe beats the best you'd expect from 15 no-skill tries, adjusted for skew and fat tails) and the Probability of Backtest Overfitting via CSCV (combinatorially symmetric cross-validation: the history is cut into 10 equal blocks and every 5-versus-5 split is tried; PBO is how often the configuration that looked best in-sample lands below the median out-of-sample). There is no purging or embargo between the blocks. The panel labels PBO of 50% or more "likely overfit", 30–50% "fragile", below 30% "holds up". Both numbers move with the sliders, so this guide does not quote them. These tests shrink apparent edge; they never create it.

Forward-volatility lab — predict what's predictable

Read it: Predictive tab. Rows: SPY's realized volatility over the next 5 and 20 days predicted from simple price-state features (out-of-sample R²), forward direction as a control, the same model with forward-macro features added, and a no-model persistence baseline. Higher R² = more of the next period's volatility explained; at or below 0 = no better than predicting the average.
Does NOT mean: NOT a price or direction forecast — direction is the control row and stays at or below zero. A learnable volatility forecast is a risk input for sizing rules you set in advance, not a signal to trade, and an R² of 0.2–0.4 still leaves most of the next period's volatility unexplained.

The constructive complement to the Alpha Lab: point the same honest machinery at what is learnable. A ridge regression (λ = 1) on three risk-state features — SPY's trailing 20-day realized volatility, the Alpha Lab's walk-forward turbulence, and the 60-day SPY–TLT correlation — is scored with blocked 5-fold cross-validation, dropping 20 days of training data on either side of each test fold (purge + embargo) so overlapping windows cannot leak.

On the embedded snapshot (markets as of 2026-06-16): forward 5-day volatility R² ≈ 0.42 and forward 20-day volatility R² ≈ 0.23, while forward direction scores −0.12 (5-day) and −0.49 (20-day) — worse than simply predicting the average. Today's volatility used directly as the forecast (the persistence baseline) scores −0.09. The 5-day reads higher than the 20-day because volatility clustering is strongest at short lags — it does not make the model "more predictive" of anything else. That contrast is the whole lesson: aim any learning at vol/regime/risk, not at price direction.

The "more data" experiment — an honest negative. A natural instinct is "add the best forward-content macro data and the model improves." The lab tests exactly that: it adds the 10y−2y yield curve, the high-yield spread, the Chicago Fed financial-conditions index, VIX and the 10-year real rate as features. The out-of-sample result is that they do not help the 20-day model — they slightly degrade it (R² 0.23 → 0.18). This is reported on the panel with a red ▼0.05, not hidden. It is the "shallow wins" / overfitting lesson demonstrated on live data: more and better data does not equal better prediction — slow, correlated features add parameters that overfit even under ridge regularization. Those macro series carry genuine signal, but at the recession / regime horizon (months), which is why they live in the Forward-macro panel — not inside a 20-day model.

Forecast reliability — the meta-learning layer

Read it: A layer above the forecasts. It does not predict — it reports when each forecast can be trusted: a skill board (out-of-sample R² for 5- and 20-day volatility vs direction) and a regime-conditional reliability table (how forecastable volatility is in calm / normal / stressed regimes), with your current regime marked.
Does NOT mean: NOT a way to manufacture directional alpha. Conditioning on regime improves your sense of how much to trust and size the volatility/risk forecast — it never makes direction forecastable. Direction stays R²≈0 in every regime.

This is meta-labeling in its only honest form (after López de Prado): use the working risk model to gate and size, not to invent a directional signal. The skill board repeats the forward-volatility lab's out-of-sample R². The regime table splits SPY's history into terciles of trailing 20-day volatility (calm / normal / stressed, about 830 days each) and, within each, reports how well trailing 5-day volatility tracks the next 5 days' volatility, and trailing 5-day return the next 5 days' return (squared correlations — in-sample, unlike the skill board).

The regime table makes two facts concrete. First, volatility is far more forecastable when stress is already elevated — its regime-conditional R² climbs from roughly 0.01 in calm markets to ~0.07 normal to ~0.35 stressed (clustering is strongest in turmoil). Second, direction is unforecastable in every regime (R²≈0 throughout). So the honest use is: in a stressed regime, give the volatility/risk reading more weight inside sizing rules you set in advance; never read any of it as a direction call, and never as a reason to trade. Overnight vs intraday — the one free directional regularity

Read it: Pick a fund (SPY, QQQ, IWM, GLD or TLT) and a window (full history or the last 3 years). The card splits each day into its overnight (close→open) and intraday (open→close) halves, plotting the cumulative return of holding only-overnight vs only-intraday vs buy-and-hold, with annualised figures. Most of the equity-index return has historically accrued overnight.
Does NOT mean: NOT a tradable edge. You cannot capture the close-to-open gap net of spreads, slippage and overnight-gap risk. It is a risk premium / opening-auction print artifact, not informational alpha, and not a forecast.

This card is the honest answer to "what free public data predicts short-term direction?" The answer is: almost nothing — except this one documented, index-level regularity (Lou-Polk-Skouras 2019; the New York Fed's overnight-drift work). On this data SPY's overnight half earns about +8.8%/yr versus +4.4%/yr intraday — roughly two-thirds of the buy-and-hold return — and small-caps (IWM) are starker still: the intraday half is actually negative, so effectively all of the return came overnight.

Why it is not a trade. The drift has persisted in this sample rather than arbitraging away — which is precisely the signature of a risk premium (you are paid for bearing overnight-gap and dealer-inventory / liquidity-provision risk) plus an opening-auction print artifact, not free informational alpha. You cannot isolate the gap without paying spreads and slippage that erase it, and you would still carry the overnight-gap risk you are being compensated for. Switch the selector to TLT (Treasuries) and the pattern inverts (about −6.4%/yr overnight vs +2.3%/yr intraday over the full history), which suggests an equity-flow effect rather than a universal law — though GLD (gold) shows the equity-like pattern too (about +13.8%/yr overnight vs −1.2%/yr intraday), so the split is not purely about equities.

It is the closest free data gets to short-term directional structure, included precisely to show why that structure is still not a forecast you can trade — the same lesson as the Alpha Lab and the volatility lab, made concrete on a real, named anomaly. The card uses its own embedded daily open/close history for these five funds (June 2016 – June 2026), which is never refreshed live; "Last 3 years" means the last 756 sessions.

Long-horizon valuation thermometer

Read it: Predictive tab. Where the US market's Shiller CAPE sits versus its own history: the latest embedded value and its percentile since 1881 and since 1950, the multiple of the long-run median, the earnings yield next to the 10-year Treasury yield (a rough equity risk premium), and the 10-year real return a simple CAPE regression implies, with a 95% band.
Does NOT mean: NOT a timing signal and NOT a forecast you can act on this year. The in-sample fit does not survive out of sample, the band is wider than the estimate, and the horizon is a decade — useless for the console's 5–20-day window, where direction is correctly ~0. Expensive markets can stay expensive, or get more so, for years.

The one place the academic canon (Cochrane's discount-rate result; Shiller's CAPE) says market direction is even weakly forecastable from free data — and only over many years. A high CAPE (low earnings yield) has gone with lower real returns over the following decade, through mean-reversion of the risk premium. It is an equilibrium risk-premium level, not a mispricing to arbitrage.

The data is Shiller's monthly CAPE series as embedded in the page — 1881 to September 2024 — and it is not refreshed, so this card lags the rest of the console (markets as of 2026-06-16) by almost two years. The honesty wrapper is the finding, not boilerplate: these predictors mostly fail to beat the historical mean out of sample (Goyal–Welch 2008/2022), beating it only weakly and only with sign/valuation constraints (Campbell–Thompson), and persistent-regressor (Stambaugh) bias inflates the in-sample fit. Cross-sectional factor demo — what "doing what quants do" returns

Read it: Predictive tab, amber border. A frozen exhibit — the result of an offline backtest, not computed in the page — of the one quant playbook free data allows: rank 85 large-cap stocks each month on a no-fit blend of three documented factors (12-minus-1-month momentum, 1-month reversal, low volatility) and go long the top fifth, short the bottom fifth. Read it top to bottom: is the signal there, does it pay after costs, does it persist, does breadth rescue it.
Does NOT mean: NOT a strategy, NOT a signal and NOT a recommendation — the finding is that there is no investable edge. The numbers are fixed; they do not update with the console's data.
  • Is the signal there? Composite rank information coefficient +0.021 (next month, out-of-sample), 95% interval −0.021 to +0.062, p = 0.33 — indistinguishable from zero; t = 1.0 against the field's discovery hurdle of t > 3.0 (Harvey–Liu–Zhu). Per signal: momentum +0.029, reversal +0.045, low-vol −0.041 (suspect).
  • Does it pay? Gross +4.6%/yr (Sharpe 0.26) shrinks to +3.2%/yr at 5 bps per side, +1.8% at 10, −1.1% at 20 and −3.9% at 30. Break-even is about 16 bps per side — below an honest all-in cost (20–30 bps of spread and impact plus about 50 bps a year to borrow the shorts). Net max drawdown −37.6%.
  • Does it persist? IC +0.042 in 2016–21 → about 0 in 2021–26 — though the decay itself is not significant, so "real edge that faded" cannot be told apart from noise.
  • Does breadth rescue it? Widening the same model from 85 hand-picked mega-caps to 589 names takes the IC to −0.003 and the gross Sharpe to −0.06 (break-even −3 bps: a loss even trading free). Breadth amplifies real signal; here there was none to amplify — Grinold's law run in reverse.

Survivorship, stated on the card: the universe is 85 current (2026-picked) large-caps, so failed and delisted names are missing. That inflates momentum and reversal and may flip low-vol's sign — the true effect is at most what is shown — while picking liquid mega-caps deflates the real premia, which live in small, illiquid names. The number is squeezed from both sides.

What it proves is epistemic: the accessible levers are honesty instruments that cleanly reject a fake edge, not machines that manufacture a real one. Professionals make a similarly tiny IC pay with what free data cannot buy — a delisting-inclusive universe, microcap access, a securities-lending desk, and execution that keeps turnover below the cost cliff.

Read it: Set your comfort band (target volatility, 5–40%, default 15%) and read the exposure your own rule implies: min(100%, your target ÷ SPY's forecast volatility), floored at 30%. Below it, a walk-forward check shows what that rule did to SPY's drawdowns, and what it cost in turnover.
Does NOT mean: NOT a direction call and NOT a buy/sell signal. It is your band applied to a volatility forecast — it only ever suggests holding less, never levers up, and earns no return. How it works. The volatility forecast is SPY's trailing 20-day realized volatility, annualized. Exposure = min(100%, your target ÷ that forecast), never below 30%: when forecast volatility is above your band the rule says hold less; when it is below, the rule says 100% — never more. Walk-forward check. Over SPY's full embedded history the card applies the rule day by day, each day's exposure using only earlier returns, and compares the maximum drawdown of buy-and-hold with that of the vol-sized version, plus the turnover the rule generates (exposure changes per year). Scenario stress-test — synthetic crises to size against

Read it: Two thousand synthetic next-month (21-trading-day) paths for SPY built from its own history: the 5th, 50th and 95th percentile of the month's return, and the worst dip within the month (median path, 5% tail, single worst path).
Does NOT mean: NOT a forecast of next month and NOT a probability you can bank on. It cannot invent a crisis worse than the moves it resamples — no novel black swan, and no 2008 (the embedded data starts in 2016). It is for stress-testing a position size you have already chosen.

Method — filtered historical simulation. Each of SPY's daily returns is divided by the EWMA volatility (λ = 0.94) prevailing at the time, giving a pool of volatility-standardized moves with the real fat tails. Each synthetic path draws 21 of those moves at random (independently, with a fixed seed) and rescales them to today's EWMA volatility. That gives a fuller range of bad months than the handful of real crises — the transparent cousin of a generative model, with no black box and no mode collapse.

Limits. Volatility is held at today's level for the whole path and the draws are independent, so the paths do not reproduce volatility clustering within the month; tails come only from the historical pool (2016 onward). A stress distribution, never a prediction.

Why it borrows the water dashboard's lenses but not its foresight

Read it: Markets have no fixed topology, no accumulation, no exogenous drivers, and no hidden state. Every structural advantage the water system has for forecasting is inverted in markets.
Does NOT mean: Do not treat elevated readings as predictive signals. They show current stress, not future moves.

The Resilience Telemetry console applies several structural analogs from the Saint Petersburg SCADA water-monitoring system (which can warn hours ahead). However, four critical market differences prevent the same forward-looking skill:

1. Fixed topology vs. estimated and rewiring

Water: The pipe network and elevation structure are ground-truth, surveyed and stable. Pressure and flow respond to known fixed constraints.
Markets: The cross-asset correlation network is estimated from noisy daily returns and systematically rewires when a crisis hits. Historical "lead-lag" dissolves; everything converges to one mode (panic). The topology is a ghost until it breaks.

2. Conservation vs. price instantaneity

Water: Water piles up in tanks of known volume and travels pipes at known speed. Total level + rate-of-rise → calculable time-to-overflow.
Markets: Nothing accumulates. A crowded position unwinds in milliseconds through price. There is no quantity to integrate forward; only the expectation of future supply/demand is priced. A crowded trade can evaporate before any aggregating metric signals it.

3. Exogenous drivers with real lead time vs. endogenous and already priced

Water: Rain and surge are exogenous — coming whether or not anyone forecasts them, and don't change course because operators reacted to yesterday's forecast.
Markets: The "drivers" (Fed policy, CPI, flows) are already priced into expectations by the time they become observable. Information arbitrage across venues means any stable upstream-leads-downstream edge is traded to lag zero. Surprise is all that remains — by definition, unforecastable.

4. Hidden-state telemetry vs. price as the expectation

Water: SCADA reads a tank level only the operator can see. The gauge is independent of what happens next; observing it does not change the water level.
Markets: Price is the aggregate expectation. There is no hidden state to telemeter. Worse, observing a gauge — discussing a risk, hedging it, trading on it — immediately changes the expectation and the price. Markets exhibit reflexivity; water systems do not.

Consequence: thermometer, not forecast

The spectral radius (ρ̂), absorption ratio, turbulence, Hawkes branching ratio, and timed lead-lag are built as a systemic-stress thermometer. By their authors' own admission, these metrics rise during 2008, 2020, and 2022 — not before. They are coincident-to-lagging measures of stress, not predictive. The only weakly-leading construct is the standardized absorption-ratio shift, and even that is sample-dependent and has not held out-of-sample.

This is exactly why everything here stays descriptive, not predictive. The console tells you where the market sits today, in geometric and spectral terms, relative to its own history — not where it will go.

FAQ for investors & operators

Read it: High readings persist for years without crashing. Lead-lag is association, not causation. Closest analogs are geometric, not predictive. Descriptive, not predictive.
Does NOT mean: This is not a black-box trading system or a crash-prediction tool. It is a diagnostic for understanding current market stress.

Q: Is a high reading (fragility ≥70) a sell signal?

A: No. A high fragility reading is a descriptive statement: "This asset's stress indicators — persistence, amplitude, tail — have been rising faster than is usual for it." It does not predict when a drawdown will occur. Readings can stay high for months or years without significant correction; conversely, crashes sometimes begin from low readings. This is a stress thermometer, not a market-timing tool.

Q: I've seen elevated readings for months with nothing happening. Does that mean the console is broken?

A: No, that is expected and honest. With only a handful of real drawdown events in a decade, the base-rate chance of a crash on any given day is already low. An elevated thermometer reading may raise that chance somewhat, but from a small number. The Backtest / base-rate honesty panel quantifies this: if you find that fragility ≥70 raises the chance of a ≥10% drawdown within the next 60 trading days from (say) 6% to 8%, that is the true signal — modest and uncertain. The console will never claim otherwise.

Q: Does an arrow in the network mean A causes B?

A: No. An arrow i→j means i's past returns line up with j's future returns more than j's past lines up with i's future (lagged cross-correlation asymmetry). It is related to — but simpler than — the "Granger causality" tests of econometrics (it does not control for j's own past), and like them it is purely correlational. Causes can include: genuine information cascades (rare), different market-open times, measurement lag, or simple overfitting. Any stable edge gets arbitraged away; what you see is mostly the noise of the week.

Q: Is the "closest-analog" state a prediction?

A: No. The diffusion map shows that today's state is geometrically closest to (say) 2019 or 2021. That historical state is a coincident analog — it looked similar. But it does not predict that the same moves will follow. Markets have short memory, regime shifts can happen overnight, and new shocks are always possible.

Q: What does it mean if spectral radius is rising?

A: It means the cross-asset correlation matrix is concentrating its variance in fewer eigenmodes. Assets are moving more in sync. This is a real, measurable signal of systemic tightness — but it is a coincident signal, visible during 2020 and 2022 as the crisis unfolded, not cleanly ahead of time. Rising coupling makes the system more fragile to the next shock, but the shock's timing and magnitude are unforecastable.

Q: If the Hawkes branching ratio approaches 1, is a crash imminent?

A: No. A branching ratio near 1 (critical coupling) means large shocks are currently self-reinforcing — they beget more large shocks. This is a real description of the shock cascade active right now. But it does not tell you how long the cascade will last, how large the next shock will be, or when it will end. You are observing the stress, not predicting its duration.

Q: What if all the indicators are red and the diffusion map shows the stressed arm?

A: That is a coherent stress description: the market is exhibiting high fragility, tight coupling, strong shock clustering, and a geometric regime similar to past crises. It is useful context for risk managers — but by the console's own rule it is not a reason to trade: review hedge ratios, concentration and liquidity assumptions on the schedule and by the rules you set in advance, not because a gauge lit up. It does not tell you to sell everything immediately, nor does it guarantee a crash by Friday. It is an honest call to heightened vigilance, not to action.

Q: Can I backtest a strategy on these signals?

A: The Alpha Lab (Predictive tab) is exactly that: a net-of-cost, walk-forward backtest of a turbulence-triggered trade with a Deflated Sharpe and PBO check — and as built it finds no edge. Backtest / research mode adds a base-rate table for fragility (conditional vs unconditional drawdown rates with event counts; no confidence intervals are drawn, so read the counts). But be warned: with a dozen real drawdown events in 10 years, apparent backtest "skill" is mostly luck. And even an edge that seemed to survive testing would not change the console's rule: no reading here justifies a trade.

Q: Is the volatility channel different from GARCH?

A: Not meaningfully. Realized volatility (the amplitude sub-score) and the bimodality coefficient mostly restate volatility clustering, which GARCH models directly. If you run a GARCH on the same asset, you will see similar conditional-vol spikes. The surprise lens (AR(1)+EWMA-vol log-likelihood) is more independent, but still a simple model. Neither the volatility channel nor surprise are strong out-of-sample predictors of drawdowns.

Q: What does the covariance shrinkage α do in the turbulence panel?

A: It blends the sample covariance matrix with a scaled identity (every variance set to the average, all correlations zero) to reduce estimation noise when data is scarce or dimensions are high. Higher α = more shrinkage. The default (0.20) is moderate. If covariance becomes ill-conditioned (nearly singular), α auto-raises to at least 0.5. Adjust the slider to see how turbulence readings change with shrinkage, but remember: turbulence itself is a coincident metric, not forward-looking.

Q: Can I use this for real-time trading?

A: No. The console is for situational awareness, not trading — including "risk-management" trades. By its own rule no reading here may justify a trade: not tightening stops, adding hedges or cutting leverage because fragility or coupling looks high ("the market looks fragile" is never a reason). If you want risk controls, write them down in advance — the Risk tab's pre-commitments and your own volatility band — and let the rule act, not the gauge. Trying to trade the direction or timing of these signals will likely fail out-of-sample.

Glossary

Read it: See definitions of all key terms: spectral radius, absorption ratio, fragility thermometer, LCCA, diffusion map, Hawkes, turbulence, surprise, DFA, bimodality, and more.
Does NOT mean: These are technical terms; understanding them is important for interpreting the console correctly and avoiding false signals.
Absorption ratio
The fraction of total variance in a correlation matrix captured by the top k eigenmodes (Kritzman-Li metric; k = 10 for the console's 48 names). High ratio = little local shock-absorbing capacity; all variance is driven by a shared global mode. Coincident, not leading.
AC1 (Lag-1 autocorrelation)
Correlation between today's return and yesterday's return. In efficient markets, ~0. Rising values suggest slower mean-reversion (critical-slowing-down analogue). Weak in returns; mostly restated by volatility clustering.
Backtest / research mode
Walk-forward computation for most panels: at any cursor date, those statistics use only data up to that date (the manifold, anticipation, the labs and the Risk cards are exceptions — see Live vs Backtest mode). The base-rate table then compares actual forward drawdowns to the base rate. Strict honesty check against luck.
Base-rate honesty
A statistical comparison of how often a ≥10% drawdown followed within 60 trading days on days when fragility was ≥70 (conditional rate) vs. on any day (unconditional base rate). If conditional ≈ unconditional, there is no demonstrated timing skill.
Bimodality coefficient
Statistical measure of "two-humped" distribution shape. Rising bimodality suggests the asset is flickering between calm and stressed states. Often a false positive driven by GARCH vol clustering alone.
Branching ratio (Hawkes)
In a Hawkes self-exciting point process, the expected number of child shocks per parent shock. Ratio ~1 = critical (approaching instability); <<1 = shocks die away fast. Realizes your LSAxJEPA temporal Hawkes λi(t).
CAPE (cyclically adjusted P/E)
Shiller's price-to-earnings ratio using the 10-year average of inflation-adjusted earnings. Weakly informative about the next decade's real returns, useless at the console's 5–20-day horizon. Embedded through September 2024.
Critical-slowing-down (CSD)
In physics, the analogue of rising recovery time, persistence, and amplification near a phase transition. These metrics (AC1, DFA, rvol, bimodality) borrow the analogy from CSD, but in markets they rise during stress, not before, and are largely coincident or even lagging.
Cross-correlation asymmetry (LCCA)
Matrix A(i→j) = D(i→j) − D(j→i), where D(i→j) is the average absolute (Fisher-z) correlation of i's past returns with j's future returns over lags 1–4 — not a Granger test. Anti-symmetric: red when i leads j, blue when j leads i. Predictive association, not causation.
CSCV / PBO
Combinatorially symmetric cross-validation: cut the history into blocks, try every half-and-half split, and count how often the configuration that won in-sample lands below the median out-of-sample. That share is the probability of backtest overfitting (PBO). The Alpha Lab uses 10 blocks, without purging.
Deflated Sharpe ratio
The probability that a strategy's Sharpe ratio beats the best Sharpe you would expect from the same number of no-skill tries, adjusted for skew and fat tails (Bailey–López de Prado). It shrinks apparent edge; it never creates it.
Diffusion map
A nonlinear dimensionality-reduction technique (generalization of spectral decomposition / PCA) that embeds high-dimensional states (here, 10-day mean returns of the 48 non-crypto names) into a low-D space so that similar regimes cluster. Realizes the geometric manifold of your LSAxJEPA framework.
DFA (Detrended fluctuation analysis) / Hurst exponent α
Measures scaling of cumulative returns; α ≈0.5 = random walk (no persistence), α >0.5 = more persistence (slow mean-reversion), α <0.5 = mean-reverting. Needs long windows; lags regime shifts; mostly descriptive.
Directedness ratio (Σ|A|/ΣD)
A network statistic: sum of absolute asymmetries divided by total lagged cross-correlation magnitude. High ratio = clear leader-follower structure; low ratio = moves are simultaneous, with no clear leader. Common (simultaneous) shocks push it down — see the common-shock drill.
Eigenvalue (λmax)
The dominant (largest) eigenvalue of a correlation matrix. Normalized by N (number of assets), it gives the spectral radius ρ̂. Dominance = systemic coherence; in crises, ρ̂ rises as assets correlate to one mode.
Filtered historical simulation (FHS)
A stress-test method: standardize past returns by the volatility of their day, resample those standardized moves, and rescale them to today's volatility. Keeps real fat tails without a black-box model; cannot produce a crisis worse than the moves it resamples.
Fragility thermometer
A per-asset 0–100 score combining three channels (persistence, amplitude, tail) built from seven indicators, measuring how steeply those indicators have been rising over the last 60 days relative to the asset's own history. High ≠ crash; can persist for years. Descriptive, not predictive.
Granger causality / lead-lag
Statistical test of whether past values of X help forecast Y more than Y's own past alone. "Lead" means X's past is predictive for Y's future; "lag" means the reverse. The console's lead-lag is the simpler LCCA asymmetry, not a Granger test. In markets, either is association driven by mechanics, measurement lag, and overfitting, not true causation.
Hawkes process
A self-exciting point process where arrivals (shocks) increase the intensity of future arrivals (branching ratio n). Fitted to daily counts of ≥2.5σ (EWMA-standardized) shocks pooled across all 51 markets; branching ratio rises in stress. Realizes your LSAxJEPA temporal shock-clustering λi(t) framework.
Ill-conditioned covariance
When a covariance matrix is nearly singular (near-zero or negative eigenvalues), inverse estimates blow up. Shrinkage α or dimension reduction is applied. The console auto-raises α to at least 0.5 if the matrix becomes ill-conditioned.
Mahalanobis distance / turbulence
One-day multivariate generalization of z-score. Measures how unusual today's cross-asset move is relative to the recent covariance. Risen in 2020/2022 (coincident), not before. An active-inference analogue (variational free energy) of per-asset surprise.
Manifold / state geometry
The high-dimensional space spanned by historical market states (here, 48-asset 10-day mean returns). A diffusion map embeds it into 2-D; similar regimes cluster. Coincident description of the market's regime geometry, not predictive of next state.
Participation ratio
The effective number of independent factors in a correlation matrix: (Σλ)² / Σλ² over its eigenvalues, i.e. trace² / ‖C‖². 48 names with a participation ratio of about 4.7 behave like fewer than five independent bets.
Permutation-gated null
Statistical test: circularly shift every series by its own random offset (160 draws) to break any lead-lag timing, recompute the asymmetries, and keep only links i→j whose asymmetry exceeds the 95th percentile of their null values. Filters spurious edges but is conservative.
Realized volatility (rvol)
The standard deviation of daily returns over a window, annualized (√(252 × variance)). A measure of magnitude of fluctuations. Rising rvol mostly restates GARCH vol clustering; a coincident metric, not predictive.
Recovery rate λ = −ln φ
Derived from AR(1) coefficient φ. λ measures the rate of exponential reversion to mean; rising λ = faster recovery. Sibling of AC1; largely redundant information in markets.
Shrinkage (α)
Regularization parameter for covariance estimation: C_shrink = (1−α)×C_sample + α×(tr C / N)×I. Higher α = more smoothing, better condition number, but less responsiveness to true covariance changes. Default 0.20; auto-raised to at least 0.5 if ill-conditioned.
Snapshot (embedded real history)
Daily closes for all 51 markets from 2016 (FX from 2015; ~2.7 years of crypto) to mid/late June 2026, plus embedded FRED macro series, open/close data for five funds and Shiller CAPE through September 2024. Baked into the HTML; always available even if live feeds fail.
Spectral radius ρ̂
The dominant eigenvalue of the rolling cross-asset correlation matrix, normalized by N. Measures systemic coupling: ρ̂→1 = tight coupling (all move together); ρ̂ low = loose coupling (independent shocks). Coincident stress metric, not predictive. Your LSAxJEPA κB analogue.
Surprise lens (active-inference analogue)
One-step negative log-likelihood of a simple AR(1)+EWMA-volatility model. Rising = today's moves are more surprising to a simple expectation model. An active-inference variational-free-energy analogue, not the full EFE engine. Per-asset, not systemic.
Walk-forward validation
Strict backtesting methodology: at any in-sample date, all parameters are estimated using only data up to that date. Forward metrics are computed on future data. Prevents look-ahead bias. The console's Backtest mode does this recomputation for its walk-forward panels (not the manifold, anticipation, the labs or the Risk cards).
